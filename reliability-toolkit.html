The Reliability Toolkit

Everything fails, all the time. Reliability isn't preventing failure — it's making sure one thing breaking doesn't take the whole cathedral down with it.

A companion to Caching, Scaling Databases, Load Balancing, and Message Queues.

◇ the domino that ate the datacenter

The recommendations service gets slow — not down, just slow. The product page calls it and waits. Threads pile up waiting. The web servers run out of threads, so they get slow. The load balancer marks them unhealthy and piles their traffic onto the survivors, which now also drown. In ninety seconds a minor hiccup in a service nobody would call critical has taken down the entire site. Nothing "broke." It cascaded. This guide is the set of tools that stop the dominoes.

At scale, something is always broken. A disk somewhere is dying, a network link is flapping, a deploy is misbehaving, a dependency is having a bad day. You cannot engineer failure away — with enough machines, the rare becomes the constant. So the goal shifts from "don't fail" to "fail small, fail contained, and keep serving what you still can." This is resilience, and it's a set of specific, learnable moves.

Two numbers worth knowing Availability is often quoted in nines. "Three nines" (99.9%) is ~8.76 hours of downtime a year (about 8h 46m); "four nines" (99.99%) is ~52 minutes; "five nines" is ~5 minutes. Each extra nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more effort — which is why "how many nines do we actually need?" is a business question, not just an engineering one.

The foundation: never have exactly one of anything critical. Two servers, replicated databases, multiple availability zones. When one dies, another takes over — failover. The load balancing guide covered the mechanism and the database scaling guide covered the replica side of it; the principle here is broader: a single point of failure is a scheduled outage you haven't been told the date of yet.

The subtlety is that failover itself must be tested. A backup that has never been failed-over to is not a backup — it's a hope. Plenty of outages are made worse by a standby that turned out to be misconfigured, out of date, or, memorably, also on fire.

◈ In the wild · AWS S3 2017 Outage On February 28, 2017, an engineer running a debugging tool typed a command that deleted far more S3 indexing servers than intended in the us-east-1 region. S3 went down for hours, which broke thousands of downstream sites and other AWS services that hard-depended on it. It highlighted the blast-radius ceiling: when services compose as hard dependencies, their availability drops to the product of all inputs.

Go back to the cold open. The root sin was waiting forever for a slow service. A call with no timeout is a promise to hang indefinitely — and hung threads are the fuel of every cascade.

A timeout says: "if you haven't answered in 2 seconds, I'm giving up and moving on." It converts an unbounded hang into a fast, catchable failure you can handle. It's one line of code and it's the highest-value cheap habit in this whole guide. The catch is picking the number: too short and you kill healthy-but-busy calls; too long and you're back to hanging. Base it on real latency percentiles (p99), not vibes.

Transient failures (a blip, a brief network drop) often succeed on a second try, so retrying is smart. Retrying naively is how you turn a small problem into an outage.

War story · the retry storm A service slows down. Every client immediately retries. Those retries double the load on the already-struggling service, so it slows further, so more calls fail, so more retries fire… a self-reinforcing avalanche that keeps the service down long after the original blip is gone. Naive retries don't help a struggling system recover — they hold its head underwater.

The fix is exponential backoff with jitter: wait 1s, then 2s, then 4s before each retry (back off), and add a random wobble (jitter) so a thousand clients don't all retry in lockstep and re-synchronize the stampede. Also cap the number of retries, and don't retry things that aren't retryable (a 400 Bad Request will be just as wrong the second time). Notice this only works because of idempotency from the queues guide — retrying a non-idempotent action is how you double-charge someone.

Backoff and jitter fix retry timing. They do nothing about retry volume, which is the half that actually kills you — see retry budgets below.

Because retries are a load multiplier, and you applied one to a system whose problem was load. If the dependency is saturated rather than blipping, every retry is extra work aimed at the thing that's already failing to keep up. Two specific traps: retries configured at several layers multiply rather than add — three at the client, gateway and service is 27 attempts at the bottom — and backoff with jitter only spreads that volume over time, it doesn't reduce it. The missing piece is a retry budget that caps retries at a small fraction of live traffic and empties itself when there are no successes to refill it, so retries switch themselves off during exactly the broad outage where they'd do the most damage.

Named after the one in your house. If a service is clearly down, why keep calling it — failing slowly, tying up threads — every single time? A circuit breaker trips after it sees enough failures and then fails instantly for a while, without even attempting the call.

The breaker does two kind things at once: it protects you (no more threads wasted on doomed calls) and it protects them (the struggling service gets breathing room instead of a beating). It's the single most important pattern for stopping cascades.

◈ In the wild · Netflix Hystrix Netflix built and open-sourced Hystrix, the library that did more than anything else to popularise the circuit-breaker pattern in microservices. It wraps each call to a dependency, trips open once that dependency's error rate crosses a threshold, and then sheds load fast instead of piling more attempts onto a service that's already struggling. Netflix has since retired Hystrix in favour of newer libraries like resilience4j, but the pattern it spread is now everywhere.
Most likely it was a gray failure — a 5% error rate, or responses that were slow but successful — and a breaker watching for a 50% error rate on explicit errors will never see it. That's the fix too: count timeouts and slow responses as failures, not just non-2xx. The other common causes are a threshold or window set too loose to trigger inside the incident's duration, and a breaker scoped per-instance across a large fleet, where each instance sees too few failures individually to cross its own threshold even though the aggregate picture is obvious. Breakers fail silently in the wrong direction, which is why "did it trip?" belongs on the postmortem checklist.

Bulkheads — named after a ship's watertight compartments. Isolate resources so a flood in one area can't sink the whole vessel. If calls to the flaky recommendations service get their own limited thread pool, they can exhaust that pool without stealing threads from checkout. One feature browns out; the ship stays afloat.

Load shedding — when you're truly overwhelmed, it's better to serve most requests well than all requests badly. So deliberately reject some traffic at the door (return a quick "try again in a moment") to keep the system from collapsing entirely. Counterintuitive but vital: sometimes saying "no" fast is the most reliable thing you can do. Shedding lives next door to the health checks and connection draining in the load balancing guide — same idea, applied at the door rather than at the pool.

🛠️ Hands-On Lab: Build a Concurrency-Limiting Middleware

Build this mini-project to implement active request limits and load shedding (protecting server thread/memory pool):

  • 1. The Slow Endpoint: Write an HTTP server in Go or Node.js with a CPU-bound slow route `/hash` that computes a bcrypt password hash (simulated 200ms latency).
  • 2. Concurrency-Limiting Gate: Implement a middleware that tracks active in-flight requests using a counter. Set a hard limit of 10 concurrent requests.
  • 3. Load Shedding: If a request arrives and the counter is at 10, reject the request immediately with an HTTP status code 503 Service Unavailable or 429 Too Many Requests.
  • 4. Run the Load Test: Use k6 to hit the route with 50 concurrent virtual users. Observe that while some requests get rejected, the p99 latency for successfully served requests remains flat around 200ms. Remove the limiter, run the test again, and watch p99 grow infinitely as requests queue up.

When a non-essential dependency fails, don't take the whole page with it — shed the feature, keep the core. The recommendations engine is down? Show a generic "popular items" list, or nothing, but still let people check out. The search ranking service died? Fall back to plain results. Users forgive a missing sidebar; they don't forgive a white screen.

The mindset Ask of every feature: "if this dependency vanishes, what's the least-bad thing we can show?" Design that fallback on purpose, before the incident. A system that degrades gracefully feels far more reliable than one that's binary — perfect or dead.

Notice what you're actually doing when you serve stale prices from a cache because the pricing service is down: you're trading correctness for availability, one feature at a time. That's the choice from the consistency and CAP guide, made concrete. Graceful degradation isn't a separate topic from consistency — it's the AP branch, chosen deliberately and scoped to the parts of the product where slightly-wrong beats not-there. Checkout totals get the other answer.

Everything above is a toolbox, and a toolbox is where reliability advice usually stops. But nobody is ever handed a ticket that says "cascading failure, please apply patterns." You get a graph that's the wrong shape and four minutes to decide. The question that matters is narrower: which flood am I looking at, and which wall stops that one? Reach for the wrong tool and you'll spend the outage carefully tuning something that was never the problem.

Cascading failure gets described in metaphors — dominoes, floods, avalanches — which makes it feel like weather. It isn't. It's a multiplication you can do on a napkin, and the reason a cascade is so fast is that the multiplier is large.

Little's Law, and why a slow dependency kills every endpoint

One formula does most of the work here: concurrency = throughput × latency. If you serve 400 requests a second and each takes 50 ms, then at any instant about 20 requests are in flight. That's it. That's the law. Now watch what happens when only the latency term moves.

concurrency = throughput × latency healthy throughput 400 req/s p99 latency 50 ms (0.05 s) concurrency 400 × 0.05 = 20 slots in use thread pool 200 slots → 10× headroom, feels generous the downstream call slows to 5 s throughput 400 req/s (unchanged — callers don't know yet) latency 5000 ms (5 s) → 100× worse concurrency 400 × 5 = 2000 slots needed thread pool 200 slots → short by 1800 time from "slow" to "down" free slots at t=0 180 arrival rate 400 req/s completions none for 5 s 180 ÷ 400 = 0.45 seconds

Latency went up 100×, so required concurrency went up 100×, and the pool you thought had ten times the headroom you'd ever need was gone in under half a second. Note that nothing crashed and no error was logged by the thing that caused it. The dependency is still returning 200 OK, just eventually.

Now the part that turns a feature problem into an outage: the pool is shared. Every request that arrives after those 180 slots are gone gets nothing — the login page, the health check, the status endpoint, the one endpoint in your service that has never once called recommendations. That's the cold open, in numbers. A slow dependency doesn't degrade the feature that uses it; it takes the whole process, because threads and connections are the currency all your endpoints spend.

The line worth memorising A timeout is a concurrency budget. Rearranged, the law says the most concurrency a dependency can ever consume is throughput × timeout. Setting a timeout of 5 seconds on a call that takes 400 requests a second isn't a latency decision — it's you authorising that call to occupy 2000 slots. Nobody would sign that if it were written down. It usually is, in a config file, as timeout: 5000.

Redo it for your own service and you'll see why timeouts alone don't save you. Drop the timeout from 5 s to 1 s and the arithmetic gives 400 × 1 = 400 slots — still double the pool. A tight timeout buys you time; it doesn't buy you a bound, because the throughput term is set by your users and not by you.

The bound is what a bulkhead is for, and the same formula sizes it. Give recommendations its own pool of 20 connections and the answer stops depending on how fast the dependency is: 20 is the most it can ever take, and everything else fails instantly at the bulkhead instead of queuing inside your process. Run it the other way — throughput = concurrency ÷ latency — and you get the shape of the degraded state: 20 ÷ 5 = 4 requests a second still get real recommendations, the other 396 get the fallback, and checkout never notices. That's the whole toolkit in one line of arithmetic: the outage became a slightly worse sidebar.

The nines multiply, and that's the problem

The other arithmetic people skip is what a chain of dependencies does to an availability figure. Availability composes by multiplication, not by averaging, and multiplication of numbers below 1 only goes one direction.

ten hard dependencies, each at three nines (99.9%) 0.99910 = 0.99004 → 99.0% , about 87 hours of downtime a year compare: three nines on its own 0.999 = 99.9% → about 8.8 hours a year

A service that depends on ten things each at three nines cannot itself be at three nines. It starts its life at 99.0% and then subtracts whatever its own bugs cost. You don't get to promise your users an availability number better than the product of everything you hard-depend on, and no amount of care in your own code changes that — you'd be trying to be more reliable than your inputs.

Two honest caveats, both of which matter. First, this assumes failures are independent, and they usually aren't: shared availability zones, a shared config push, a shared identity service. Correlated failure makes the real number worse than the arithmetic, not better. Second, it assumes every dependency failure becomes your failure — and breaking that assumption is precisely what this entire guide is about. A timeout with a designed fallback converts a dependency's outage into a degraded response instead of an outage of your own, which takes that dependency out of the product entirely.

The number actually worth tracking Not "how many dependencies do I have" but "how many hard dependencies do I have" — the ones with no fallback, where their bad day is definitionally your bad day. Count them. That count is your availability ceiling, and the useful reliability work is nearly always moving items off that list rather than making each one slightly better.
Availability composes by multiplication, so four hard dependencies at three nines put your ceiling at 0.999⁴ ≈ 99.6% — around 35 hours a year — before your own code contributes a single bug. You cannot be an order of magnitude more available than your inputs. The sentence is only rescuable if those four aren't hard dependencies: if each one has a timeout and a designed fallback, its outage becomes a degraded response rather than yours, and it drops out of the product entirely. That's the real point — the fix isn't making four services better, it's moving them off the hard-dependency list.

The five walls are the part that fits on a slide. What follows is the part that shows up in postmortems.

Retry budgets: backoff controls timing, not volume

Exponential backoff and jitter decide when retries happen. Nothing in either decides how many. During a broad outage, every client is failing, so every client is retrying, and politely staggered retries are still a multiplied traffic load arriving at a service that is already on the floor. Backoff spreads the stampede over a few seconds; it does not make it smaller.

The volume control is a retry budget: a token bucket, refilled by successful requests, that permits retries only up to a small fraction of live traffic — on the order of 10%. The property this gives you is the one you want: when a few calls fail, retries are free and they work. When everything fails, there are no successes refilling the bucket, the budget empties, and retries simply stop. The system loses its ability to amplify its own outage at exactly the moment that ability would be most destructive.

Pair it with the other rule: retry at exactly one layer. Three retries in the client SDK, three in the API gateway, and three in the service is not nine attempts — it's 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 requests hitting the bottom of the stack for one user action, generated by three teams who each individually did the responsible thing. Pick the layer that knows enough to judge whether a retry is safe, retry there, and make every other layer fail fast. The queues guide has the other half of this: retries are only safe on operations that are idempotent.

Deadline propagation: a timeout set per hop is a lie

Every service in the chain sets its own timeout, independently, based on its own sensible reasoning. The caller waits 1 second. The service it calls waits 3. So one second in, the caller has given up, returned an error to the user, and moved on — while the callee is still working, still holding a connection, still hammering the database, producing an answer that will be written to a socket nobody is reading. Under load, a meaningful share of your capacity goes to computing responses that are already garbage.

The fix is to pass the remaining deadline down with the request — a header, a context object, whatever your stack calls it — and have each hop check it before starting work and refuse anything it can't plausibly finish in time.

POST /checkout           deadline = now + 1000ms
  └─ POST /inventory     remaining: 940ms   → ok, proceed
       └─ SELECT ...     remaining: 780ms   → ok, proceed
  └─ POST /recommend     remaining: 120ms   → p99 is 300ms
                                            → refuse now, return fallback
                                            (don't start work nobody will wait for)

This is what makes timeouts compose. Without it you have a pile of unrelated numbers that happen to be in the same request path; with it you have one budget, spent down the stack, with every hop able to see how much is left.

Metastable failure: the outage that outlives its cause

This is the most important idea missing from most reliability writing, and it's the one that produces the 3 a.m. phone call nobody can explain. Some systems, once pushed past a threshold, stay collapsed after the original trigger is gone. The deploy was rolled back twenty minutes ago. The slow query was killed. The dependency has been green for a while now. The service is still down.

The reason is that the outage manufactured its own new load. The queued retries from every client that failed during the incident are still arriving. The caches went cold while the service was unavailable, so every request that does get through is now a full cache miss and costs ten times what it used to. Connection pools are churning. That combination — retry backlog plus cold cache — is, by itself, more load than the system can serve, which produces more failures, which produces more retries. The trigger is long gone; the feedback loop it created is self-sustaining.

The counter-intuitive part You cannot wait a metastable failure out. Patience is the wrong instinct and it is the instinct everyone has. Recovery requires actively removing load — aggressive shedding, flushing retry queues, sometimes turning traffic off entirely and reintroducing it in slices while caches refill. You will be deliberately taking down a service that is already down, in front of an audience, at 3 a.m. Nobody makes that call well under those conditions, which is exactly why it has to be decided, written down, and rehearsed in advance. A 2021 HotOS paper gave this failure mode its name — metastable failure — and naming it is most of the battle, because a team that has the word will recognise the shape in minutes instead of hours.

Gray failure: nothing is ever cleanly dead

Every diagram in this guide implies a binary — up or down, healthy or failed, closed or open. Real outages are almost never that considerate. A node that is slow. A node failing 5% of requests. A node that answers GET /health perfectly and cannot serve a single real query because the connection pool to its database is exhausted. A node that looks healthy to the load balancer sitting next to it in the same rack, and unreachable to the clients in another region.

Each of these evades a binary health check, which is why gray failures last so much longer than clean ones: your automation sees nothing wrong and does nothing, while your users see everything wrong. The defences are behavioural rather than binary — health checks that exercise the real dependency path rather than returning a constant, latency-based ejection rather than error-based, breakers that count slow responses as failures, and outlier detection that compares a node against its peers instead of against a threshold.

The line to keep Every network partition is, in practice, a timeout you configured. No packet ever arrives announcing a partition. There is only silence, and a number you chose that decides how long silence is allowed to last before it counts as failure. Which means the boundary between "slow" and "dead" isn't a property of the network — it's a product of your config file.

Fail open or fail closed is a product decision

The auth service is down. Does the door open, or does it stay shut? Fail open and you're serving users you can no longer authenticate. Fail closed and you're locking out every legitimate customer over a dependency they've never heard of. The fraud checker is down — do you take the payment, or refuse the money? There is no engineering answer to either. There is a revenue answer, a legal answer, and a trust answer, and they don't all point the same way.

Two things follow. First, someone with the authority to make that trade-off must decide before the incident, because during the incident the decision falls to whoever is on call, who does not have that authority and is guessing. Second, the right answer differs per feature within the same system: the session-validity check on a "recently viewed" widget can fail open without anyone caring, and the same check on the admin panel absolutely cannot. Treating fail-open as a system-wide switch is how you end up with one setting that's wrong somewhere important.

Circuit breaker tuning is where breakers go wrong

The state machine is the easy part; nobody gets that wrong. The thresholds are where breakers earn their reputation for being more trouble than they're worth.

Set them too tight — trip on 3 failures, or on a 10% error rate — and the breaker opens on ordinary background noise. You've now cut yourself off from a dependency that was fine, and you'll do it repeatedly, converting a service that was 99.5% available into one you refuse to call. Set them too loose — 90% errors over a five-minute window — and it never opens at all during the incident it existed for. Anything with a 5% error rate, which is most gray failures, will never cross a threshold set on error rate alone. And a breaker evaluated over too few requests trips on statistical noise: require a minimum volume in the window, or a quiet endpoint will open a circuit on the strength of two unlucky calls.

Then half-open, which is the state people forget to tune. When the cooldown expires, the recovering service must be probed by a trickle — a handful of requests, one at a time. If every instance in your fleet resumes full traffic at the same moment the cooldown ends, you have built a synchronised stampede aimed at a service with cold caches and empty pools, and you will knock it straight back down. The same jitter that fixes retry timing belongs on breaker cooldowns for exactly the same reason.

And then the human factors, which cause more downtime than any of this

Every mechanism in this guide is executed by tired people reading a screen at 3 a.m. That's the actual runtime environment, and it's the one least often designed for.

The runbook nobody has run

Written once, at the end of a project, by someone who has since left. It references a dashboard that was renamed and a script that requires permissions the on-call doesn't have. A runbook is a hypothesis until someone has followed it start to finish.

The dashboard nobody can read

Sixty panels, no ordering, and nothing that answers "is it us or is it them?" in the first five seconds. At 3 a.m. you get one glance. If the top of the page doesn't say what's broken, the dashboard is decoration.

Alerts too noisy to hear

Every page that didn't need a human trains the next one to be ignored. Alert fatigue isn't laziness — it's a correctly learned response to a channel with a bad signal rate, and the real alert dies in that noise.

The unrehearsed rollback

"We can always roll back" is a claim about a procedure nobody has performed under pressure. Then it turns out the migration wasn't backward-compatible, and you discover this during the incident rather than during a Tuesday.

Recovery paths that only run in emergencies

Code that executes exclusively during disasters gets tested exclusively during disasters. Failover, fallback, restore, shed — anything on that list which hasn't run this month should be assumed broken.

The fix: make them routine

The most reliable systems aren't the ones with the best failure handling. They're the ones whose failure handling runs constantly — failover as a weekly exercise, restores on a schedule, breakers that trip in normal operation and nobody notices.

Which is the honest argument for the next section. Chaos engineering isn't about breaking things for sport — it's the mechanism that moves a recovery path from the "only runs in emergencies" column to the "runs every Tuesday" column. That's the entire value proposition.

◈ In the wild · Knight Capital rollback In 2012, trading firm Knight Capital deployed new logic to 8 server instances, but one server didn't receive the update due to a manual deploy mismatch. The old code remained active, leading to automated stock purchases that lost the firm $440M in 45 minutes. This incident highlighted the necessity of automated rollbacks, deployment validation, and peer outlier detection.
This is a metastable failure: the outage created its own load — a backlog of client retries plus caches that went cold while you were unavailable — and that load alone is now enough to keep you down. The original trigger is irrelevant; you're stuck in a feedback loop it started. Waiting will not fix it, because nothing in the loop decays on its own. You escape by actively removing load: shed aggressively, drop or drain the retry backlog, and if necessary take traffic to zero and reintroduce it in slices so caches can refill against a survivable request rate. Yes, that means deliberately taking down something already down — which is why it should be a written, pre-approved procedure rather than a judgement call at 3 a.m. There is no engineering answer — it's a product decision about which risk the business prefers, and it must be made per feature rather than per system. Failing open on a session check for a "recently viewed" widget costs nothing; failing open on the admin panel or a payment authorisation is how you end up explaining yourself to a regulator. Failing closed on everything is safer and may still be the wrong answer if it means zero revenue for the duration. The engineering obligation is to surface the choice to someone with the authority to make it, in advance, and to build the switch so it can actually be flipped per feature during an incident — not to guess at 3 a.m.

Here's the punchline to all of the above: a failure path you've never triggered probably doesn't work. Backups you don't restore, failovers you don't rehearse, circuit breakers you've never watched trip — they're all theories until tested. So the boldest teams break things on purpose, in production, during business hours, to prove the safety nets hold.

◈ In the wild · Netflix Chaos Monkey After a painful 2008 database outage and the move to AWS that followed, Netflix built Chaos Monkey — part of a wider toolkit it called the "Simian Army" — to randomly terminate production instances during business hours. The point was to force every service to actually tolerate a sudden instance vanishing rather than merely hope it would, turning resilience into something continuously tested instead of quietly assumed.

Netflix pioneered this with Chaos Monkey, a tool that randomly kills live servers to force engineers to build systems that don't care when a server dies. If a random instance vanishing is a non-event, then a real one dying at 3 AM is also a non-event — and everyone sleeps. Chaos engineering is just the discipline of finding your weaknesses before they find you.
  • Redundancy — answers "one machine died." Useless until you've actually failed over to the spare.
  • Timeout — answers "it's slow." Bounds the wait, and by doing so bounds the concurrency: throughput × timeout.
  • Retry with backoff + jitter — answers "it blipped." Fixes retry timing only.
  • Retry budget — answers "everything blipped at once." Fixes retry volume, which is the half that causes outages.
  • Circuit breaker — answers "it's down." Stops paying for calls you know will fail, and gives the sick service room. Says nothing about overload.
  • Bulkhead — answers "one thing is eating everything." The only tool that gives you a hard bound rather than a smaller number.
  • Load shedding — answers "there's more work than machine." Says nothing about a slow dependency.
  • Graceful degradation — answers "this part is optional." Decide the least-bad fallback before the incident, per feature.
  • Deadline propagation — makes the timeouts above compose instead of contradicting each other.
  • Chaos engineering — the only evidence that any of the above works.
  • Metastable failure — the outage that survives its own cause. Shed load to escape; you cannot wait it out.

99%3.65 days of downtime a year — about 7.3 hours a month. "Two nines" sounds respectable and is roughly a working day per quarter.
99.9%8.76 hours a year — about 43.8 minutes a month. The default target most teams claim without checking.
99.95%4.38 hours a year — about 21.9 minutes a month. A common contractual middle ground.
99.99%52.6 minutes a year — about 4.4 minutes a month. Less than one bad deploy's worth. Requires automated recovery; humans are too slow to be in the loop.

2 retriesStarting point for a retry cap — three attempts total, at exactly one layer, only on idempotent operations.
≈10%Starting point for a retry budget: retries capped at roughly a tenth of live traffic, with the bucket refilled by successes.
≈50% / 20 reqsStarting point for a circuit breaker: trip at about a 50% error rate, but only once the window holds at least ~20 requests, so a quiet endpoint can't trip on two unlucky calls.

Read that table correctly The availability rows are arithmetic — they're true regardless of what you're building. Everything below them is a starting point to measure against, not a benchmark to hit. A batch job and a payment API have no business sharing a timeout, and anyone who quotes you a universally correct retry count is quoting you a number they didn't measure.
