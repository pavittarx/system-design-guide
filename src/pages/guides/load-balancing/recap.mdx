<Recap>

- **The job** is to answer "which backend?" correctly, instantly, millions of times a second — and to notice when the answer stops being a backend at all.

- **L4 forwards packets, L7 reads requests.** Content routing, TLS termination, header rewrites, per-route limits and cross-backend retries are all L7. Protocol neutrality, source-IP fidelity and minimum latency are all L4. Edge L4 in front of a tier of L7 proxies is a very common shape.

- **Round robin is blind to work.** A backend taking 100× longer gets the same number of requests. Prefer least-connections, and implement it as [power of two random choices](/guides/load-balancing/failure/#p2c) so it survives a tier of load balancers.

- **Consistent hashing** is what stops a fleet size change from invalidating every cache key at once.

- **Sticky sessions are a band-aid.** Push session state into a shared cache and every server becomes interchangeable — which is also what sharding in [the Database guide](/guides/database-scaling/) assumes.

- **Liveness ≠ readiness.** "Restart me?" and "send me traffic?" are different questions. A static 200 answers neither.

- **Removal is not free.** Drain first, or every deploy is an error spike. And the traffic you remove lands on instances that may not have room for it.

- **Health checks can fail open.** If every host looks unhealthy, the likeliest bug is in the checker. Know what your proxy does at zero healthy hosts.

- **Retries multiply.** Retry at one layer, cap it with a budget, jitter the backoff.

- **Only refusal is fast enough.** Autoscaling takes minutes; a cascade takes seconds. Shed load and cap concurrency per instance.

</Recap>

And a handful of numbers worth carrying around. These are **order-of-magnitude starting points, not benchmarks** — every one of them should end up tuned to your own request profile, and none of them is a target to hit.

<Table numeric>

| Number | What it is | Why roughly that |
| ------ | ---------- | ---------------- |
| 5–10s | Health-check interval | Fast enough to catch a death within one page load's patience; slow enough that a 500-instance fleet isn't a DDoS against its own health endpoint. |
| 2–3 fails | Unhealthy threshold | One failed check is noise. Two or three is a signal. Combined with the interval this sets your detection time: 3 × 10s ≈ 30 seconds to eject. |
| 2 passes | Healthy threshold | Coming back should be easier than going away, but not instant, or a flapping instance oscillates in and out of rotation. |
| 30–120s | Deregistration delay / drain timeout | Longer than your slowest ordinary request, shorter than your appetite for a rolling deploy. AWS defaults to 300s, which is usually far more than a web tier needs. Long-poll or streaming endpoints need more. |
| 30–300s | Slow-start ramp for a new instance | Long enough to fill pools and warm caches under gradually increasing real traffic. Match it to how long your p99 actually takes to settle after a restart. |
| ~10–100µs | Added latency, L4 hop | Forwarding with no parsing, often in kernel space or on the NIC. Effectively free next to a network round trip. |
| ~0.5–2ms | Added latency, L7 hop | Parse, route, re-emit, plus buffering. Small in absolute terms, and material only when your own p50 is single-digit milliseconds or you're chaining several proxies. |
| 1–5ms | TLS handshake CPU at the terminator | Per new connection, not per request — which is exactly why connection reuse and session resumption matter so much at the edge. |
| 10–20% | Retry budget, as a share of traffic | Enough headroom to paper over ordinary blips; low enough that a total outage cannot multiply offered load. Envoy defaults to 20%. |

</Table>
