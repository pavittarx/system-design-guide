<Collapse id="cacheable" title="1. Ask how stale it can be, not whether it's static">

Your shop's product listing page gets 5,000 requests a minute at lunchtime, and every one of them makes your servers query the database and render the same HTML. The page changes maybe twice an hour. The usual advice says the CDN is for images, CSS and JavaScript, and "dynamic" pages like this go to your servers. That advice was true around 2005 and has been costing people performance ever since.

### The better question

Instead of "is this static?", ask of each response: **how out of date can this be, and for whom?** Ask it per response and the old categories fall apart:

<Table>

| Content | How stale it can be | Where it can live |
| ------- | ------------------- | ----------------- |
| JS/CSS with a hash in the name (`app.9f2c1a.js`) | Forever: a new version gets a new name | Edge, one year, `immutable` |
| Product images | Days | Edge |
| Product listing page | Seconds to minutes | Edge, short expiry |
| Price / stock level | Seconds | Edge, very short expiry, plus purge on change |
| Personalised homepage | Zero for the personal parts | Cached shell, with the personal bits added at the edge |
| Cart contents | Zero | Origin, never cached |
| Anything sent with POST | Not applicable | Origin |

</Table>

How long a cached copy counts as fresh is its **TTL** ("time to live"), set by your `Cache-Control: max-age=…` header. Once the TTL runs out, the next request makes the PoP fetch a new copy from your origin.

### Why ten seconds of staleness is worth so much

Back to the listing page. Cache it at the edge for just **ten seconds**. Within each ten-second window, the first request at a PoP goes to your origin and everyone else gets the copy. So at one PoP the page is generated at most 6 times a minute instead of 5,000: a 99.9% cut in origin work, for staleness nobody will ever notice.

<Note type="key" label="Short TTLs are far more powerful than they look">

$$
\text{origin fetches per minute} \;\le\; \frac{60}{\text{TTL in seconds}} \quad \text{per PoP, per object}
$$

That holds *however much traffic arrives*. A 10-second TTL means at most 6 origin fetches a minute for that object at one PoP, whether it's requested a hundred times or a hundred thousand. Your traffic and your origin load stop being connected at all.

Going from 10 seconds to 60 only takes you from 6 to 1. The first ten seconds bought almost all of the benefit, which is why "we can't cache, our data changes constantly" is nearly always an argument for a short TTL, not for none.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="cachekey" title="2. How one header can empty your cache">

Your hit rate was 95%. Marketing starts a campaign, and a week later it's 40%, though the pages haven't changed at all. To see why, you need to know how a CDN decides that two requests are asking for the same thing.

### The cache key

For every request, the CDN builds a label from parts of it, by default roughly the host, the path and the query string. Requests with the same label get the same cached copy. This label is called the **cache key**. Every distinct key is a separate cached object, fetched from your origin separately and stored separately. So every extra thing that goes into the key divides your hit rate.

### Query strings: the usual culprit

Marketing adds tracking parameters to links. Now `/shoes`, `/shoes?utm_source=email`, `/shoes?utm_source=twitter&utm_campaign=spring` and ten thousand other variations are ten thousand separate objects. Each is fetched from origin at least once, each takes up cache space, and each has a hit rate near zero, for a page that is byte-for-byte identical every time.

The fix is to clean up the key, which is called **normalising** it: strip parameters that don't change the response, sort the ones that do (`?a=1&b=2` and `?b=2&a=1` are different strings, so different keys), and drop anything you don't recognise.

### Vary: telling the cache to keep separate copies

A response can carry a `Vary` header, which tells caches "the right answer also depends on this request header, so store a separate copy for each value of it". Whether that's harmless or a disaster depends entirely on how many different values the header has:

<Note type="warn" label="Vary multiplies your copies, often by thousands">

`Vary: Accept-Encoding` is fine. There are a handful of values, and you genuinely need separate gzip and brotli copies.

`Vary: User-Agent` is catastrophic. There are effectively unlimited user-agent strings: every browser build, every OS patch level, every crawler gets its own copy of every object. Hit rates collapse from 95% to single digits, and the origin finds out what its real traffic looks like.

`Vary: Cookie` is worse, because every user has their own session cookie. The cache is now per-user and does nothing except fill disks.

</Note>

How many distinct values something can take is called its **cardinality**. That one word is the difference between a useful key and a catastrophic one. Adding a `country` value from geolocation gives you three or five copies and correct regional pricing. Adding the raw client IP gives you one copy per user. It's the same lesson as metric labels in [Seeing Inside the Machine](/guides/observability/), for the same reason.

### When a caching mistake leaks someone's account

Cookies can also make a cache *wrong*, not just slow. A response that logs someone in carries a `Set-Cookie` header with their session. Suppose someone has switched off the CDN's default refusal to cache those, to push the hit rate up:

<Race a="Alice" b="Bob" result="Result: Bob is now logged in as Alice" steps={[

Nothing crashed, so no alert fires. CDNs refuse to cache responses with `Set-Cookie` by default for exactly this reason; the danger arrives when someone overrides that default to fix a hit-rate problem. The safe pattern is to serve personalised and cacheable content from different URLs, so no configuration mistake can ever mix them up.

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="shield" title="3. Stopping 100 PoPs from all asking at once">

This problem only appears once the CDN is working well. You have a hundred PoPs, all holding a popular object. Its TTL runs out. Here's what two of them do:

<Race a="Sydney PoP" b="Frankfurt PoP" result="Result: the origin fetches the same bytes twice, and with 100 PoPs, 100 times" steps={[

  { b: "TTL expires. Next request is a miss" },

  { b: "Fetches the identical object from your origin", tone: "bad" }
]} />

The PoPs don't talk to each other, so each one asks your origin. An origin that was serving nothing suddenly gets a hundred simultaneous requests for the same bytes. Multiply that by every object expiring in that minute, and the CDN you bought to protect your origin has become a denial-of-service attack pointed at yourself.

### Put one PoP in charge of talking to you

An **origin shield** is a single chosen PoP (or a small set of them) that sits between all the other PoPs and your origin. Edge PoPs never talk to your origin directly. They ask the shield, and only the shield asks you. The shield is usually placed near your origin, so its hop to you is short and its connection stays warm.

<Note type="key" label="The shield arithmetic">

100 PoPs, one object, TTL expires. Without a shield: **100 origin fetches** for the same bytes.

With a shield: each PoP misses to the shield, the shield fetches **once**, and the other 99 are answered from the shield's copy.

Origin sees **1** request instead of 100, a 99% cut from nothing but the layout of the network.

</Note>

Why this works: it's the single-flight idea from [Understanding Caching](/guides/understanding-caching/), applied one layer up. When many requests miss on the same key at once, send one upstream and make the rest wait for its answer. Good CDNs also do this **within** a PoP, which is called **request coalescing**: a thousand users hitting one cold PoP produce one request to the shield, not a thousand. The two combine. A thousand users per PoP across a hundred PoPs, a hundred thousand requests, becomes one origin fetch.

### The cost: an extra hop on misses

A Sydney user whose PoP misses now waits for Sydney → shield (say, Virginia) → origin, instead of Sydney → origin. If the shield sits next to the origin, the extra time is small and only paid on misses. Put the shield in the wrong place, say Europe when your origin is in Oregon, and every miss takes a scenic detour across two oceans.

### More layers, same idea

**Tiered caching** extends this into layers: edge tier, regional tier, shield tier, origin. Each layer up has fewer, larger caches. A rarely requested file that falls out of a small edge cache can still be a hit one layer up. It's a memory hierarchy, and it obeys the same rules as the one in your CPU.

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- Decide per response how stale it may be. Even a 10-second TTL caps origin fetches at 6 a minute per PoP, whatever the traffic.
- Everything in the cache key, including every `Vary` header, splits your cache. Keep what goes in it to a handful of values, and never cache a `Set-Cookie` response.
- Without a shield, every PoP fetches from your origin separately. A shield and request coalescing collapse those into one.

[Part 2](/guides/cdn-edge/freshness/) is about taking a cached answer back.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="You lower a TTL from 3600s to 10s across your whole catalogue. What actually happens to origin load?" options={["Roughly unchanged, because the hit rate stays above 99%", "It rises 360× per PoP per object, multiplied across every PoP and object, which can be an outage", "It falls, because fresher objects need fewer revalidations"]} answer={1}>

Per PoP and per object, origin requests go from 60 ÷ 3600 ≈ 0.017 a minute to 60 ÷ 10 = 6 a minute. That's 360 times more, and it's multiplied by your number of PoPs and your number of objects. On 100 PoPs and 10,000 objects it's 6 million origin requests a minute. That isn't a TTL change; it's an outage.

The fixes, in order: an origin shield, so 100 PoPs collapse to 1; `stale-while-revalidate` (see [Part 2](/guides/cdn-edge/freshness/#stale)), so refetches happen in the background instead of while a user waits; and random variation in expiry times, so they don't all land in the same second. With all three, a short TTL is genuinely affordable, and that's what makes near-real-time content work on a CDN.

</Quiz>

<Quiz q="Your hit rate is near 0%, cache usage is huge, and database load is pinning. You see Vary: User-Agent in the headers. What's the arithmetic?" options={["Every distinct User-Agent string gets its own copy, so hundreds of thousands of variants each miss", "User-Agent changes on every request, so the CDN refuses to cache the response at all", "The CDN caches one copy but revalidates it with origin for every new browser"]} answer={0}>

User-Agent strings differ for nearly every browser build, OS version and device: hundreds of thousands of distinct values in real traffic. `Vary: User-Agent` tells the cache to keep a separate copy for each one. You get a huge number of near-identical objects, a hit rate near zero, storage costs that look like a bug, and an origin hit as if the CDN weren't there.

If you really need different markup for mobile and desktop, have the edge turn the User-Agent into a small header like `X-Device: mobile|desktop|tablet` and vary on that: three copies instead of three hundred thousand. Better still, serve one responsive page to everyone and let CSS handle it.

</Quiz>

<Quiz q="Your hit rate is 94% and your origin bill went up anyway. What are the two most likely causes?" options={["The shield tier counts its hits twice, so the real hit rate is lower", "Origin egress is billed per request, so the 6% of misses cost more each month", "The misses are the large objects, so the byte hit rate is far lower; or a cache key change split objects into many variants"]} answer={2}>

First: hit rate counts *requests*, but bandwidth bills count *bytes*. If the 6% of misses are your big files (video segments, large images, uncompressed downloads), a 94% request hit rate can be a 50% byte hit rate. Always look at both.

Second: a cache key change split your cache. Someone added a query parameter, a `Vary` header or a country dimension, and now each object exists in many copies, each fetched from origin once per PoP. The hit rate can stay high overall while origin fetches multiply, because the total number of requests grew too.

</Quiz>
