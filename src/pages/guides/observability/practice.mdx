<Collapse id="incident" title="1. One incident, solved with three signals">

Go back to the [cold open](/guides/observability/) that starts this guide: checkout is broken, every dashboard is green, and the team loses forty minutes. Here's the same incident again, with the whole toolkit used the way it's meant to be used. Each step uses the signal that's cheap for that job.

<Stack>
  <Layer idx="1" title="The page" speed="seconds">Not "CPU high". The symptom alert fires: checkout success rate has fallen below the SLO threshold, and the one-hour burn rate is 20×. Something a customer would notice is happening now.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="2" title="Metrics: draw a box around it" speed="~90 seconds">Request rate normal. Error rate 0.02%, unchanged, so requests aren't failing. p50 latency flat at 20 ms, but p99 up from 240 ms to 2.4 seconds. Slice the p99 by region: all regions. By instance: all instances. By endpoint: only checkout.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="3" title="Traces: find where" speed="minutes">Pull the slow traces; tail sampling kept them all. Every one has the same shape: `pricing.quote` holding 2.3 seconds, and `fx.rates` inside it burning 2.27 of those across three attempts, two of which timed out.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="4" title="Logs: find why" speed="minutes">Filter the currency service's structured logs by trace ID. Every timed-out call has `upstream: "fx-provider-eu"` and a connect time of exactly the 750 ms timeout. Group by `deploy_version`: it began at 09:02, when the service picked up a config change that moved its provider endpoint to an address that now resolves to a different continent.</Layer>
</Stack>

### What each step ruled out

Step 2 is worth slowing down on. A flat p50 with a p99 up tenfold means most requests are fine and a minority are very slow: a problem in the **tail** of the distribution, not a broken system. Four slices then ruled out a bad host and a bad region and left one path, checkout. No code was read and nothing was redeployed.

Step 3 answered *where* (the [trace in Part 2](/guides/observability/logs-traces/#traces) is this incident), but not *why*. Step 4 supplied the why: a new endpoint on another continent made every connection slower than the timeout, and physics did the rest.

<Note type="key" label="The shape to internalise">

Metrics *bounded* the problem in about ninety seconds. Traces *located* it. Logs *explained* it. Each signal did the job it's cheap at, and none was asked to do a job it's bad at. The forty minutes in the cold open were spent asking metrics a question only traces could answer.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="gotchas" title="2. Eight ways your instruments mislead you">

Each of these looks like the system telling you something. Each is actually the instruments telling you something about themselves.

**Missing data looks like zero.** A service dies and stops reporting. Its error-rate graph goes flat, not up, so an alert on "errors above a threshold" never fires: there are no errors, there's nothing at all. Alert on absence explicitly, and give your alerting a **heartbeat**, a rule that fires if it hasn't heard from itself recently. A monitoring system that goes down silently is worse than none, because you still trust it.

**Your scrape interval is your resolution limit.** A gauge read every 15 seconds can't see a 3-second spike. Queue depth, connection-pool exhaustion and garbage-collection pauses all happen faster than that. If a gauge is flat while users time out, suspect the reading interval before you suspect the users.

**Fast failures make latency graphs improve.** If a bug turns 30% of traffic into instant 500 errors, your p99 gets *better*, because instant errors are the fastest requests you have. Always split latency by outcome, or your main graph will mislead you during the exact incident it exists for.

**Auto-scaled y-axes make everything dramatic.** When the axis stretches to fit the data, a 0.3% wobble fills the panel and reads as a catastrophe. On a fixed axis, a real doubling reads correctly. Pin the axis to something meaningful, and draw the SLO threshold as a line so the eye has a reference.

**Clock skew scrambles traces.** Each span carries a timestamp from the machine that produced it. If one machine's clock is 400 ms off, its child span appears to start before its parent, and the waterfall stops making sense. Keep clocks synced with **NTP** (the standard clock-sync protocol) and graph each machine's offset; it's a two-line metric that will one day save you a day.

**Averaged percentiles, one more time.** A panel showing `avg(p99)` displays a number that matches no property of your system. Add the histogram buckets together, then compute the percentile once.

**Logs hold whatever you put in them, including what you shouldn't.** Email addresses, tokens, full request bodies with card details. Logs are usually the least access-controlled data store in the company and the most widely copied. Redact in the logging library where the line is written, not in a pipeline downstream, because someone debugging will bypass the pipeline eventually.

**You only see the paths you own.** A request that never reached your service produces no telemetry in it. Failed TLS handshakes, DNS problems, gateway 502 errors and client-side errors are all invisible from the inside. An SLI computed from your application logs can't count the requests that never arrived, and those are exactly the ones customers complain about.

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- Use metrics to narrow the problem down, traces to find where, logs to explain why.
- Flat can mean "fine" or "no data"; alert on missing data explicitly.
- Split latency by outcome, and measure as close to the user as you can.

</Note>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="During an incident your p99 latency graph improved. What's the most likely explanation?" options={["The incident shed load, so the remaining requests had spare capacity", "Autoscaling added instances faster than traffic grew", "Something started failing fast, and the quick errors pulled the latency distribution down"]} answer={2}>

Something started failing fast. A dependency that refuses a connection in two milliseconds is far quicker than one doing real work. A bug that turns a third of your traffic into immediate 500 errors pulls the whole distribution down, and the graph looks better than yesterday.

The same happens when a circuit breaker opens: the breaker is working correctly, but latency alone can no longer tell you about health. Split every latency metric by outcome, make the main panel show successful requests only, and put the error rate right beside it. A graph that mixes successes and failures blends two different populations into one misleading number.

</Quiz>

<Quiz q="Your SLI is computed from application logs and shows 99.99% success. Customers report frequent failures. What could the SLI be missing?" options={["Requests that failed before reaching the application: TLS, DNS, gateway errors", "Requests that were sampled out of the logs", "Requests slower than the p99"]} answer={0}>

An application can only log requests that reached it. A failed TLS handshake, a DNS problem or a 502 from the gateway never gets that far, so it never appears in the denominator or the failures. The SLI measures the service's view of itself, not the user's experience.

Compute the SLI as close to the user as you can: at the load balancer, or from the client itself. That's the same reason an SLO should be measured from the user's side.

</Quiz>
