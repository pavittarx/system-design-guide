<Collapse id="slo" title="1. Turning &quot;reliable enough&quot; into a number">

Product wants to ship a risky feature on Friday. The on-call engineer wants to hold it, because last week was rough. Both sides have a point, and without a number nobody can settle it. Everything so far has been about seeing; this section turns what you see into a decision.

### Three terms, one idea

First decide what "working" means for one request, and measure it. For example: the share of checkout requests that succeeded in under 300 ms. That measurement is called a **service level indicator**, or **SLI**.

Then set a target for it: 99.9% of requests, measured over 30 days. The target is a **service level objective**, or **SLO**.

A **service level agreement**, or **SLA**, is an SLO with money attached and lawyers involved. It should always be looser than your internal SLO, so you're paging yourself well before you're paying customers.

<Note type="key" label="What 99.9% over 30 days actually buys you">

30 days = 43,200 minutes. 0.1% of that is **43.2 minutes** of allowed failure.

At 10,000 requests/second, 30 days is 25.92 billion requests, so the allowance is **25.92 million failed requests**.

Add a nine (99.99%) and it's 4.32 minutes. Take one away (99%) and it's 7.2 hours. Each nine is roughly ten times more engineering, forever, which is why "let's just do five nines" should be met with a cost estimate rather than a nod.

</Note>

### The failures you're allowed are a budget

Here's the clever part. If your SLO is 99.9%, you are *permitted* 0.1% failure. That allowance is called the **error budget**, and you can spend it: on risky deploys, migrations, chaos experiments, an aggressive launch. Reliability stops being a vague virtue that competes with shipping and becomes a quantity both sides can point at.

Back to Friday. Budget left? Ship. Budget gone? Freeze features and spend the sprint on stability. Agree that policy *before* the budget runs out, or it isn't a policy: it's a negotiation you'll lose during an incident.

### Two choices that make it honest

**Measure from the user's side**: at the load balancer or in the client, not inside the service. Otherwise you systematically miss the failures where the request never reached you.

**Pick a window and stick to it.** A rolling 30-day window is standard. Calendar months create an unhelpful reset on the 1st: the team that burned everything on the 29th is suddenly flush again two days later.

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="alerting" title="2. Page on what users feel, not on what might cause it">

An alert should mean one thing: a human needs to do something, now. Everything else belongs on a dashboard, in a ticket or in a weekly report. Most alerting fails because it was built the other way round, paging on every cause anyone could think of, and the result is noise.

### Noise trains people to ignore pages

Sixty alert rules, each with one false alarm a month, is two pages a day, forever. Nobody stays sharp under that. The team learns, correctly, that pages are usually nothing, and the response time on the page that *is* something quietly stretches from three minutes to twenty.

<Note label="Symptom vs cause, concretely">

**Cause alert:** "database CPU is above 90%." Might be fine. Might be a batch job. Wakes someone at 3 a.m. to look at a graph and go back to bed.

**Symptom alert:** "checkout success rate is below 99% and falling." Always matters. Fires for database CPU, and for the seventeen other things you didn't anticipate.

</Note>

A **cause** is something that might hurt users. A **symptom** is users actually being hurt. Page on symptoms.

### Ask how fast the budget is burning

"Are we failing right now?" is the wrong question for a page. The useful one is "at this rate, will we blow the budget?" The speed you're using up the budget is called the **burn rate**.

Burn rate is your current error rate divided by the error rate your SLO allows. With a 99.9% SLO you're allowed 0.1% errors:

- **0.1% errors** is a burn rate of 1: you'll use exactly your budget over the 30 days.
- **0.15% errors** is a burn rate of 1.5: the budget runs out on day 20.
- **1.44% errors** is a burn rate of 14.4.

A burn rate of 14.4 for one hour uses 2% of the whole 30-day budget. Why: one hour is 1/720 of thirty days, about 0.139%, and 14.4 × 0.139% ≈ 2%. That's the standard fast-burn page: severe enough to be real, short enough to catch quickly.

### A fast rule for pages, a slow rule for tickets

Pair the fast rule with a slow one routed to a ticket, not a page. The Google SRE Workbook suggests a burn rate of 1 over three days, which is 10% of the budget. The fast rule catches the outage. The slow rule catches the steady 0.15% error rate that no single moment makes urgent but that will empty the budget by the 20th.

Each rule should check two windows at once: a long one and a short one. The Workbook's fast page fires only when the burn rate is over 14.4 across both the last hour *and* the last five minutes. A thirty-second blip can't trip the hour, and once errors stop, the five-minute window clears the alert within five minutes instead of an hour.

<Note type="warn" label="The 3 a.m. denominator problem">

At peak, a 5% error rate is thousands of failures and unambiguously real. At 3 a.m. you serve two requests, one fails, and your ratio-based alert reports a **50% error rate** and pages someone. Every rate-based alert needs a minimum-volume floor: fire only if the error ratio is breached *and* the count of failures is above some threshold. Otherwise your alerting is loudest exactly when traffic is quietest.

</Note>

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- An SLO turns reliability into a budget of allowed failures: 99.9% over 30 days is 43.2 minutes.
- Page on symptoms users feel, not on causes that might matter.
- Alert on burn rate across a long and a short window, with a minimum-volume floor for quiet hours.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="Your metrics pipeline dies at 2 a.m. Every graph goes flat and no alert fires. Why, and what would have caught it?" options={["Threshold rules have no value to compare once data stops, so absence looks like calm; a staleness rule and an external dead man's switch catch it", "The thresholds were too high for night-time traffic; a minimum-volume floor would have caught it", "The alert manager throttles pages overnight; a second notification channel would have caught it"]} answer={0}>

Almost every alert is a threshold on a value: errors above N, latency above M. When the data stops arriving there's no value to compare, so the rule evaluates to nothing rather than to a breach. Absence looks like calm.

Two mechanisms cover it. A **staleness rule** fires when a series you expect hasn't reported for some multiple of its scrape interval. A **dead man's switch** is an alert wired to fire *continuously* into an outside service, which pages you if the stream of alerts *stops*.

The second catches a total pipeline failure, because it doesn't need the pipeline to be alive. The general principle: anything that monitors itself has a blind spot exactly the size of itself.

</Quiz>

<Quiz q="Your SLO is 99.9% over 30 days. For the last hour, 1.44% of requests have failed. What should happen?" options={["Nothing yet; 1.44% is a small error rate", "Page someone: that's a burn rate of 14.4, which uses 2% of the monthly budget in one hour", "Open a ticket for the next working day"]} answer={1}>

Your SLO allows 0.1% errors, so 1.44% is 14.4 times the allowed rate: a burn rate of 14.4. One hour is 1/720 of the 30-day window, so an hour at that rate uses about 2% of the whole month's budget. If it continues, the budget is gone in about two days.

That's exactly the fast-burn threshold the Google SRE Workbook recommends paging on. The number that looks small, 1.44%, is only meaningful against the budget, which is the whole point of burn-rate alerting.

</Quiz>
