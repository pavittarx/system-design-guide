<Collapse id="slo" title="1. SLIs, SLOs and error budgets">

Everything so far has been about seeing. This section is about deciding what "working" means, in a number, in advance — which is the part that turns telemetry into a management tool rather than a hobby.

An **SLI** (service level indicator) is a measurement: the proportion of requests that succeeded in under 300 ms. An **SLO** (objective) is a target for it: 99.9% of requests, measured over 30 days. An **SLA** is an SLO with money attached and lawyers involved, and should always be looser than your internal SLO — you want to be paging yourself well before you're paying customers.

<Note type="key" label="What 99.9% over 30 days actually buys you">

30 days = 43,200 minutes. 0.1% of that is **43.2 minutes** of budget.

At 10,000 requests/second, 30 days is 25.92 billion requests, so the budget is **25.92 million failed requests**.

Add a nine and it's 4.32 minutes. Take one away and it's 7.2 hours. Each nine is roughly an order of magnitude more engineering, forever — which is why "let's just do five nines" is a sentence that should be met with a cost estimate rather than a nod.

</Note>

The **error budget** is the inversion, and it's the genuinely clever idea: if your SLO is 99.9%, you are *permitted* 0.1% failure. That budget is a resource. Spend it on risky deploys, migrations, chaos experiments, an aggressive feature launch. Reliability stops being a vague virtue that competes with shipping and becomes a quantity that both sides of the argument can point at.

Budget remaining? Ship. Budget exhausted? Freeze features, spend the sprint on stability. The policy has to be agreed before the budget is gone, or it is not a policy — it is a negotiation you will lose during an incident.

Two design decisions determine whether an SLO is useful or theatre. First, **measure from the user's side**: at the load balancer or in the client, not inside the service, or you will systematically exclude the failures where the request never arrived. Second, **pick a window and stick to it**. A rolling 30-day window is standard; calendar-month windows produce an unhelpful psychological reset on the first of the month, where the team that burned everything on the 29th is suddenly flush again on the 1st.

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="alerting" title="2. Alert on symptoms, not causes">

An alert should mean: a human needs to do something, now. Everything else is a dashboard, a ticket, or a weekly report. Most alerting systems fail because they were built on the opposite principle — page on every cause anyone could think of — and the result is not vigilance but noise.

The arithmetic of noise is brutal. Sixty alert rules, each with one false positive per month, is two pages a day, forever. Nobody stays sharp under that. The team learns, correctly and rationally, that pages are usually nothing, and the response time on the page that *is* something quietly stretches from three minutes to twenty.

<Note label="Symptom vs cause, concretely">

**Cause alert:** "database CPU is above 90%." Might be fine. Might be a batch job. Wakes someone at 3 a.m. to look at a graph and go back to bed.

**Symptom alert:** "checkout success rate is below 99% and falling." Always matters. Fires for database CPU, and for the seventeen other things you didn't anticipate.

</Note>

The refinement on top of symptom alerting is **burn-rate alerting**, which asks not "are we failing right now" but "at this rate, will we blow the budget." A burn rate of 1 means you'll consume exactly your budget over the SLO window.

A burn rate of 14.4 sustained for an hour consumes 2% of a 30-day budget — because an hour is 1/720 of thirty days, or about 0.139%, and 14.4 × 0.139% ≈ 2%. That combination is the standard fast-burn page: severe enough to be real, short enough to catch quickly.

Pair it with a slow-burn rule — something like a burn rate of 3 sustained over six hours — routed to a ticket rather than a page. The fast rule catches the outage. The slow rule catches the steady 0.5% error rate that no single moment makes urgent but that will exhaust your budget by the 20th. Requiring both a short and a long window to be in violation before firing is what keeps a thirty-second blip from waking anyone.

<Note type="warn" label="The 3 a.m. denominator problem">

At peak, a 5% error rate is thousands of failures and unambiguously real. At 3 a.m. you serve two requests, one fails, and your ratio-based alert reports a **50% error rate** and pages someone. Every rate-based alert needs a minimum-volume floor: fire only if the error ratio is breached *and* the absolute count of failures exceeds some threshold. Otherwise your alerting gets loudest exactly when your traffic is quietest.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="Your metrics pipeline dies at 2 a.m. Every graph goes flat and no alert fires. Why, and what would have caught it?" options={["Threshold rules have no value to compare once data stops, so absence looks like calm; a staleness rule and an external dead man's switch catch it", "The thresholds were too high for night-time traffic; a minimum-volume floor would have caught it", "The alert manager throttles pages overnight; a second notification channel would have caught it"]} answer={0}>

Because almost every alert is written as a threshold on a value — errors above N, latency above M — and when a series stops arriving there is no value to compare, so the rule evaluates to nothing rather than to a breach. Absence looks like calm.

Two mechanisms cover it: an explicit staleness rule that fires when a series you expect has reported nothing for some multiple of its scrape interval, and a dead man's switch, which is an alert deliberately wired to fire continuously into an external service that pages you if the stream *stops*.

The second is the one that catches a total pipeline failure, because it does not depend on the pipeline being alive to work — and the general principle it embodies is that anything monitoring itself has a blind spot exactly the size of itself.

</Quiz>

</Collapse>
