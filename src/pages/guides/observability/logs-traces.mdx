<Collapse id="logs" title="1. Logs: structured, sampled, expensive">

Logs are the signal everybody starts with and almost nobody plans for. The failure mode is not that they don't work — it's that they work fine at 100 requests per second and become a budget line item at 10,000.

Work the numbers. Ten thousand requests per second, one log line per request, 1.2 KB per line: that is 12 MB/s, or roughly **1 TB per day**, from one service. Ingest-and-index pricing for hosted log platforms is commonly quoted in the region of a few tenths of a dollar per GB, so a terabyte a day lands somewhere in the mid five figures per month — for one service, before anyone adds debug logging to chase a bug and forgets to take it out.

<Note type="warn" label="The debug-logging ratchet">

Every incident ends with someone adding more logging so that next time they'll have the data. Nobody's incident ever ends with someone removing logging. Log volume only goes one direction unless you actively push back, and the push-back has to be someone's explicit job.

</Note>

**Structured logging** is the single highest-leverage change available. A log line like `Failed to process order 88213 for user 4471 after 3 retries` is readable by a human and opaque to a machine — you cannot filter it, group it, or count it without writing a regex that will break the next time someone rewords the message.

The same event as JSON, with `event`, `order_id`, `user_id`, `retry_count` and `duration_ms` as fields, is queryable. You can ask "show me the p95 retry count grouped by shard for the last hour" and get an answer in seconds.

Once logs are structured, the line between "logs" and "metrics" blurs usefully. A sufficiently rich structured event, with high-cardinality fields attached, lets you compute the metric you wish you'd defined — after the fact, during the incident. That is the core argument for event-based observability: rather than deciding in advance which aggregations matter, keep the events and aggregate on demand.

**Sampling** is how you make that affordable, and the naive version is a trap. Sampling 1% of all logs uniformly means you keep 1% of your errors too — and errors are rare and precious. The workable pattern is *dynamic* sampling: keep 100% of errors, 100% of slow requests, 100% of anything touching a customer you care about, and 0.1% of the boring successful ones. Record the sampling rate on every retained event so you can multiply back up and still get correct totals.

<Note label="Three fields worth putting on every line">

A **trace ID** (so a log can be joined to the trace it belongs to), a **request ID** (so a customer's support ticket can be tied to actual events), and the **deploy or build version** (so "did this start with the release?" takes ten seconds to answer rather than forty minutes).

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="traces" title="2. Traces: spans and propagation">

Metrics told you the checkout endpoint is slow. Logs told you what one request did inside one service. Neither tells you that the slowness lives three hops down, inside a call your service makes to a service that calls a service. That's what tracing is for.

A **trace** is one request's complete journey. A **span** is one unit of work within it — a service handling the request, a database query, an outbound HTTP call. Spans nest: the parent span's duration contains its children's.

What makes it work is **context propagation**: the trace ID is passed along with every outbound call, so that work happening in a different process on a different continent still knows which request it belongs to. The W3C `traceparent` header is the standard way to carry it, and OpenTelemetry is the standard way to produce it.

<Note type="wild" label="◈ In the wild · Google Dapper">

Tracing a request across a fleet of services this way is not a new idea — it is essentially Google's Dapper, described in a [2010 paper](https://research.google/pubs/dapper-a-large-scale-distributed-systems-tracing-infrastructure/). Dapper stamps every incoming request with a trace ID and carries it along each service call and RPC the request touches, exactly the propagation described above, and it samples aggressively so the overhead stays negligible. Zipkin, Jaeger, and the OpenTelemetry tooling most teams reach for today are all descendants of that design.

</Note>

```trace
trace 4f9a2c…  POST /checkout  **2,410 ms**
├─ gateway                     **2,410 ms**
│  ├─ auth.verify                 12 ms  ++ok++
│  ├─ cart.get                    31 ms  ++ok++
│  ├─ pricing.quote            ~~2,301 ms~~  ~~← the whole problem~~
│  │  ├─ tax.lookup               18 ms  ++ok++
│  │  └─ fx.rates              ~~2,270 ms~~  ~~3 attempts, 2 timed out~~
│  │                                     ~~at 750 ms each~~
│  └─ order.create                58 ms  ++ok++
```

Read that trace and the diagnosis writes itself: the currency service is timing out, the pricing service is retrying it three times, and the retries are serial. Nobody's error rate went up — every one of those spans eventually succeeded — which is precisely why the metrics dashboard stayed green. The retry policy converted an availability problem into a latency problem, and latency problems hide better.

<Note type="key" label="Traces make retry cost visible, and almost nothing else does">

A downstream service with a 5% timeout rate and three retries looks perfectly healthy from both ends: the caller succeeds, the callee returns 200s. The cost lands entirely in the tail of someone else's latency distribution. See [The Reliability Toolkit](/guides/reliability-toolkit/) for why the retries are configured that way and what to do instead.

</Note>

**Sampling** is unavoidable — storing every span of every request at scale is prohibitive — and where you decide is the interesting question. *Head sampling* makes the keep-or-drop decision at the start of the request, before anything has happened. It's cheap and simple, and it throws away the interesting requests at exactly the same rate as the boring ones.

*Tail sampling* buffers all the spans and decides after the request completes, so you can keep every trace that errored or exceeded a latency threshold and 0.1% of the rest. It costs memory in the collector and adds a delay before traces are queryable, and it is almost always worth it.

The other half of the value is structural rather than diagnostic: a trace is an empirical, always-current service dependency graph. The architecture diagram in the wiki is a claim about how the system works; the trace data is a measurement. They differ more often than anyone is comfortable with.

<Collapse id="cardinality" title="3. Cardinality: the bill that ate the budget">

Time-series databases store one series per unique combination of metric name and label values. Not one per metric — one per *combination*. That word does all the damage.

Start with something reasonable. `http_requests_total` labelled by endpoint, status, region and instance:

<Note type="key" label="The multiplication">

20 endpoints × 5 status classes × 3 regions × 100 instances = **30,000 series**. Entirely fine.

Now someone adds `user_id`, because per-user latency would be useful. With 2 million users: 30,000 × 2,000,000 = **60 billion series**. Your metrics backend either falls over or bills you as though you asked for that, because you did.

</Note>

The insidious part is that the explosive labels rarely look explosive. `user_id` is obvious in hindsight. Less obvious: a raw URL path, where `/orders/88213` and `/orders/88214` are different label values and every order in your database becomes a permanent time series. Or an error message string, where a message containing a timestamp or an ID generates a new series per occurrence.

Or a `pod_name` label in a Kubernetes cluster that redeploys twenty times a day, quietly adding a fresh set of series each time and never retiring the old ones — *churn*, the cardinality problem's slower cousin, where nothing looks alarming at any instant but the retained series count climbs all month.

<Note type="warn" label="The rule">

A metric label must be **bounded and small** — ideally under a hundred possible values, and never a value that comes from user input or a database identifier. If you want to slice by something unbounded, that is a job for logs, traces, or exemplars, not for a label. Anything you'd need a `WHERE id =` for belongs in an event, not a metric.

</Note>

The right answer to "I want per-customer latency" is usually not a metric at all. It's structured events with the customer ID as a field, queried on demand — the cost is storage that scales with traffic rather than a cross-product that scales with your customer table. **Exemplars** are the useful bridge: a histogram bucket carries a handful of sample trace IDs, so you can click from "the p99 bucket got fatter" straight to an actual slow trace, without ever putting a high-cardinality value in a label.

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="You sample 1% of traces at ingress to control cost. Why do rare errors stay invisible?" options={["1% is simply too low; at 10% the rare errors would show up reliably", "Errored requests don't propagate the trace context, so their spans never join a trace", "The keep-or-drop decision is made before the request runs, so errors are dropped at the same rate as everything else"]} answer={2}>

Head sampling decides before the request runs, so it cannot know the request will be interesting — it keeps 1% of everything, which means 1% of your errors too. An error occurring in one request per hundred thousand now appears roughly once per ten million, which in practice means never, and when you finally get a report you'll find you have no trace for it.

Tail sampling fixes this by buffering all spans until the request completes and deciding afterwards: keep 100% of errors, 100% of anything over a latency threshold, and a fraction of a percent of the routine successes. The costs are real — the collector needs memory to hold in-flight traces, and traces become queryable a few seconds later than they otherwise would — but they buy you the property that the traces you kept are the ones you'd actually want, which is the entire point of keeping any.

</Quiz>

<Quiz q="Product wants per-customer latency, and you have 50,000 customers. Do you add a customer_id label?" options={["Yes, 50,000 values is small for a modern time-series database", "No; put customer_id on structured events or traces and query on demand, with a bounded label if you need alerting", "Yes, but only on the latency histogram, not on the request counter"]} answer={1}>

No — that multiplies every existing series by 50,000 and will either take down your metrics backend or produce a bill that gets the whole observability programme cancelled. The correct home for an unbounded dimension is an event store: structured logs or traces with `customer_id` as a field, queried on demand, where cost scales with traffic rather than with the cross-product of every label.

If you need alerting rather than ad-hoc querying, the usual compromise is to promote a bounded subset — a `tier` label with three values, or an explicit allow-list of your twenty largest accounts, which keeps the cardinality fixed and known. Exemplars close the remaining gap by attaching a few trace IDs to each histogram bucket, so you can jump from an aggregate to a specific customer's slow request without any high-cardinality label existing at all.

</Quiz>
