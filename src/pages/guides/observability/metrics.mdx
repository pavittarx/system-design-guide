<Collapse id="metrics" title="1. Three kinds of number, three ways to mislead">

A metric is just a number your service reports over and over. There are three kinds, and picking the wrong one gives you a graph that's worse than no graph, because it looks authoritative.

### The number that only goes up

You add a metric for requests served. The graph shows 4,812,331 and climbing. Is that good? There's no way to tell: the number mostly reflects how long the process has been running.

A metric that only ever increases, like requests served, bytes written or errors returned, is called a **counter**. You never read its value directly. You read how fast it's growing: "312 requests per second over the last minute". That's its **rate**.

Why restarts don't break it: when a process restarts, its counter drops back to zero. Query functions such as Prometheus's `rate()` spot the drop, treat it as a reset and keep counting from zero, instead of drawing a huge negative spike.

### The number that misses whatever happens between looks

Some things go up and down: queue depth, connections in use, memory, temperature. A metric like that is called a **gauge**, and the collector reads it at intervals, typically every 15 seconds. That interval is called the **scrape interval**. Whatever happens between two readings is invisible.

<Race a="Collector (reads every 15 s)" b="The queue" result="Result: a flat line at zero, while users caught in the burst timed out" steps={[
  { a: "00:00 Reads queue depth: 0" },
  { b: "00:05 A burst arrives. Depth spikes to 50,000", tone: "bad" },
  { b: "00:08 Consumers catch up. Depth back to 0" },

]} />

This blind spot is common. Your queue-depth graph is flat and your users are timing out, because the queue filled and drained in three seconds between two readings.

### The number that tells you how slow

To know how long requests take, you need more than one number per reading. A **histogram** sorts each request into **buckets** by duration: how many took under 5 ms, under 10 ms, 25 ms, 50 ms, 100 ms, 250 ms, 500 ms, 1 s. It's how you get latency distributions, and it's the type most people use least well.

<Note type="warn" label="Histogram buckets are chosen in advance, forever">

Those buckets tell you how many requests fell in each range, and nothing more. If your real p99 is 1.4 seconds, every request above 1 s lands in one overflow bucket with no upper edge. Prometheus's `histogram_quantile` then reports the top real edge, 1 s, as your p99. It will be confidently wrong.

Pick buckets that bracket the latencies you actually care about, and revisit them when the service changes.

</Note>

The payoff for that awkwardness is large: histograms **add up**. Four hundred instances each report "this many requests under 100 ms, this many under 250 ms…". Sum the counts bucket by bucket and you have the whole fleet's distribution, so the fleet-wide p99 you compute from it is genuinely correct.

That works because you're adding counts, not combining summaries. Pre-computed percentiles can't do it, which leads to the most common statistical error in this field.

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="average" title="2. Why the average lies">

A service handles 100 requests. Ninety-nine finish in 20 ms. One takes 2 seconds. Your dashboard shows the average.

<Note type="key" label="The arithmetic">

Mean = (99 × 20 ms + 1 × 2,000 ms) ÷ 100 = **39.8 ms**.

The dashboard shows a healthy 40 ms. One user in a hundred waited two seconds. At 10,000 requests per second, that "one in a hundred" is **100 people every second**, 360,000 per hour, having a visibly broken experience that your main latency graph can't show.

</Note>

### Percentiles: line them up and read off a position

Instead of averaging, sort every request time from fastest to slowest and read the value at a given position. The value that 99% of requests beat is the **99th percentile**, or **p99**. The middle one is the **p50**, or **median**.

Here are ten request times, in milliseconds, already sorted:

```trace
  12  14  15  15  16  18  19  21  24  ~~900~~

  mean  (sum 1,054 ÷ 10)            **105 ms**  ~~nobody had this~~
  p50   5th of 10                    16 ms   ++typical user++
  p90   9th of 10                    24 ms
  p99   10th of 10 (the slowest)  ~~900 ms~~  ~~unluckiest user~~
```

The mean, 105 ms, describes nobody: nine people were under 25 ms and one waited almost a second. The percentiles describe real people. p50 is the typical experience, p99 is your unluckiest 1%, and p99.9 is where the pathological cases live: cold caches, garbage-collection pauses, retries, the one shard that's rebuilding an index.

### You can't average percentiles

Instance A reports a p99 of 100 ms and instance B reports 300 ms. The fleet p99 is not 200 ms. It isn't any function of those two numbers.

Why: a percentile is a position in a sorted list, and you can't rebuild the combined list from two positions. If B served 1% of the traffic, the fleet p99 sits close to 100 ms; if B served 60%, it's much nearer 300 ms. The only correct method is to add the histogram buckets together and compute the percentile once. Any dashboard showing "average p99 across hosts" is showing a number with no meaning.

### Your users see worse than your p99

A single page load might make 20 backend calls. If each one has an independent 1% chance of being slow, the chance that all 20 are fast is 0.99 multiplied by itself 20 times, so the chance that *at least one* is slow is:

$$
1 - 0.99^{20} \approx 0.18
$$

About **18%**. Your service's p99 is one request in a hundred; your page's is nearly one load in five. That's why per-request percentiles flatter you, and why measuring at the edge, where the user actually is, tells a different and more honest story than measuring inside one service.

### When the load test lies: coordinated omission

Your load test says p99 is 40 ms. Production says 900 ms. Before you blame production, check how the load generator sends requests. Most send one, wait for the reply, then send the next. Watch what happens when the server freezes:

<Race a="Load generator" b="Real users" result="Result: the test records one slow sample; real traffic would have produced thousands" steps={[
  { a: "Sends a request. The server freezes for 2 seconds" },
  { a: "Waits for the reply. Sends nothing else meanwhile", note: true },
  { b: "Keep arriving throughout the freeze. Every one queues", tone: "bad" },
  { a: "Gets one reply at 2 s. Records one slow sample", tone: "bad" },
  { b: "Each queued request waited up to 2 s", tone: "bad" }
]} />

The generator quietly stopped testing at exactly the moment the system was in trouble. Real users don't pause while you recover. Gil Tene named this **coordinated omission**: the generator coordinates with the system it's meant to stress.

The fix is to measure each request against the time it *should* have been sent on the intended schedule, not the time it actually went out. HdrHistogram exists largely to make that correction possible.

<Note type="war" label="Coordinated omission">

Gil Tene's name for the most insidious measurement bug there is. A load generator that waits for each response *stops sending while the system is stalled*, so a stall yields one slow sample instead of thousands. Reported p99s can read 50 ms when the truth is seconds.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="red-use" title="3. What to measure on day one: RED and USE">

You're launching a new service and have an hour for instrumentation. Two short checklists cover most of what you need, one for things that handle requests and one for things that get used up.

### RED: for anything that handles requests

For each endpoint, measure three things:

- **Rate**: requests per second.
- **Errors**: how many of them failed.
- **Duration**: the distribution of how long they took, as a histogram.

This is called the **RED** method. If you instrument nothing else, instrument these, because almost every symptom a user can notice shows up in one of them.

### USE: for anything that can run out

Two disks. One is 100% busy. The other is 70% busy, with 200 operations waiting in line. Which one is in trouble?

The second. The first is busy but keeping up: nothing is waiting, so it's simply working. The second can't keep up, and every waiting operation is a request getting slower.

That's Brendan Gregg's **USE** method, for resources such as CPUs, disks, network links, connection pools and thread pools:

- **Utilization**: the share of time it's busy.
- **Saturation**: how much work is queued, waiting for it.
- **Errors**: how often it fails.

His point is that saturation, not utilization, is the signal that matters.

<Note type="key" label="Saturation is the one people forget">

Utilization tops out at 100% and then stops telling you anything: every level of overload beyond that looks identical. Saturation keeps counting: queue depth, pool waiters, run-queue length, load average. When utilization is pinned at 100%, saturation is the only signal still saying *how much* trouble you're in, and the only one that shows the problem growing.

</Note>

### Cover both views

Google's **four golden signals**, latency, traffic, errors and saturation, are RED plus saturation, and as good a starting list as any. Which acronym you adopt matters less than covering both views. A service can fail because it's broken, which RED shows, or because something underneath it has run out, which USE shows, and the two need completely different responses.

<Note label="One refinement worth making early">

Split latency by outcome. A fast failure and a fast success are both fast. If an outage turns 30% of your traffic into instant 500 errors, a mixed latency graph *improves*. More than one team has watched p99 drop during an incident and briefly felt good about it.

</Note>

<Lab
  title="Build a RED Metric Prometheus Exporter"
  tip="Put this local metric exporter setup on your portfolio as a demonstration of production-grade RED monitoring."
>

Build this mini-project to implement custom instrumentation and scrape setups:

- **The Server:** Write an API server in Node.js or Python. Create endpoints `/checkout` (simulated 50ms latency) and `/hash` (simulated 200ms latency).

- **The Metrics Endpoint:** Expose a `/metrics` route that outputs Prometheus-formatted metrics. Implement:

  - A counter for request rates: `http_requests_total{method="POST", handler="/checkout", status="200"}`
  - A bucketed histogram for latency durations: `http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{le="0.05", handler="/checkout"}`

- **Prometheus Scrape Configuration:** Spin up a Prometheus instance in Docker with a `prometheus.yml` file configured to scrape your server's `/metrics` endpoint every 5s.

- **Benchmarking under Load:** Run a load test using `k6` against your API server. Query your local Prometheus web console at `localhost:9090`, and calculate the exact p99 latency dynamically using:

  ```promql
  histogram_quantile(0.99, sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[1m])) by (le))
  ```

</Lab>

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- Counters are read as a rate; gauges miss anything between readings; histograms are the only type that adds up correctly across a fleet.
- Use percentiles, not averages, and never average percentiles: add the histogram buckets, then compute once.
- For services, watch rate, errors and duration; for resources, watch saturation, because utilization stops at 100%.

</Note>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="Your queue-depth gauge is flat at near zero, but consumers are visibly falling behind and users are timing out. Where's the gap?" options={["The queue fills and drains between scrapes, so every sample happens to catch it nearly empty", "The gauge reports a rate rather than a current value, so the backlog is smoothed away", "The consumers are the bottleneck, so the queue really is empty and the problem is downstream"]} answer={0}>

A gauge is read at intervals, so a 15-second scrape only ever sees snapshots 15 seconds apart. A queue that fills to 40,000 and drains in four seconds is invisible: every reading happens to catch it empty.

For anything bursty, use counters instead: total enqueued and total dequeued. The gap between them reveals the backlog even between readings. Or report a high-water mark, the highest value since the last reading, which the application resets each time.

The general lesson: your scrape interval is the shortest event you can see, and many interesting ones (garbage-collection pauses, pool exhaustion, lock contention) are shorter.

</Quiz>

<Quiz q="Two instances report p99s of 100 ms and 300 ms. What's the fleet-wide p99?" options={["200 ms, the average of the two", "300 ms, the worse of the two", "It can't be known from those two numbers; you need the combined histogram buckets"]} answer={2}>

It can't be known. A percentile is a position in the sorted list of all requests, and two positions don't let you rebuild the combined list. If the 300 ms instance handled 1% of traffic, the fleet p99 is close to 100 ms; if it handled 60%, it's much nearer 300 ms. With skewed traffic, the true value can even fall outside the range of the two inputs.

The only correct method is to add the histogram bucket counts from every instance and compute the percentile once. That's exactly why bucketed histograms are worth their fixed boundaries. A dashboard showing `avg(p99)` or `max(p99)` across hosts is showing a number with no defined link to user experience.

</Quiz>

<Quiz q="Your load test reports a p99 of 40 ms; production is at 900 ms with the same traffic shape. Name the most likely measurement bug." options={["Production hardware is noisier, so every request runs slower there", "Coordinated omission: the load generator stopped sending requests while the system stalled", "The load test's histogram buckets were too coarse to record the tail"]} answer={1}>

Coordinated omission. A generator that sends a request, waits for the reply, then sends the next one stops sending exactly when the system stalls. A two-second freeze becomes one slow sample, instead of the thousands of slow requests that real users, who don't wait politely, would have produced. The tail of the reported distribution is understated by orders of magnitude.

The fix is to measure each request against the time it *should* have been sent at the intended rate, not the time it actually went out. HdrHistogram's correcting recorders do exactly this. Before rebuilding a service to chase a gap like this, check that the two numbers measure the same thing.

</Quiz>
