<Recap>

- **Numbers first.** Orders per second, reads per order, size of an order, data per year, and how long the customer may wait. A design defended without them is a guess.

- **Split what must finish now from what must finish soon.** Take the money, record the order, return. Everything else is a message on a queue.

- **Make every server interchangeable.** If no server keeps anything the next request needs, you can load-balance, autoscale and deploy one at a time.

- **The reads are usually the scaling problem.** People view 120 pages per order, so caching matters far more than sharding.

- **Indexes before replicas, replicas before sharding, and sharding probably never.** Name the number that forces each step.

- **Two writes with nothing tying them together will disagree after a crash.** Put your own events in the order's transaction (the outbox); send an idempotency key to someone else's API.

- **Decide consistency field by field.** The stock you display can be a little stale; the stock you decrement can't.

- **A timeout is a limit on how many requests can pile up.** A bulkhead caps what one dependency can use; a breaker stops you calling one that's down; shedding is for too much traffic, not for slow dependencies.

- **Pass the deadline along, cap retries, and retry at one layer only.**

- **A queue with no spare capacity above its arrival rate never recovers.** Alert on consumer lag, not queue depth.

- **Real incidents are components doing exactly what they were configured to do**, in a situation nobody pictured. The settings deserve more attention than the diagram.

- **Every addition needs a signal.** No number, no component.

</Recap>
