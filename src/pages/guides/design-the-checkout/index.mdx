<ColdOpen tag="◇ the order that was never placed">

The card was charged. The customer has the bank notification on their phone, timestamped 14:02. Support has their order history open, and it's empty. Somewhere between "payment succeeded" and "order saved", the process died. Those were two separate writes with nothing tying them together, so the system landed in the one state nobody designed for: money taken, nothing owed. There's no clever recovery now. There's only the decision, made months earlier, about whether those two facts could ever disagree.

</ColdOpen>

<Collapse id="brief" title="1. Turning a vague brief into numbers">

"Design the checkout for an e-commerce site." That's the whole brief. Someone immediately suggests Kafka; someone else wants to shard the database. Neither of them knows how many orders a second we're talking about, and without that number nobody can say who's right.

That's the most common failure in system design, in interviews and in real projects: arguing about tools before doing the arithmetic. So ask for the numbers, or assume them out loud.

### Working out the traffic

Start from what you're told and derive the rest. We're told 1 million orders on an ordinary day, and Black Friday runs 5× that. Spread over a day's 86,400 seconds, a normal day is only about 12 orders a second. But traffic isn't spread evenly: the busiest hour of the busiest day holds about 12% of its orders.

$$
\frac{5{,}000{,}000 \times 12\%}{3{,}600 \text{ s}} = \frac{600{,}000}{3{,}600} \approx 170 \text{ orders/s}
$$

In plain words: at the worst moment of the worst day, about 170 orders arrive every second. We'll design for 200, because the number you design for shouldn't be the number you predicted.

The other number that shapes everything: people look far more than they buy, about 120 page views per order. So at peak, reads are 200 × 120 = 24,000 a second, call it 25,000. That's two orders of magnitude more than the writes, and it decides where the real work is.

Here is every number we'll design against. Each one is used later:

<Table numeric>

| Number | What it is | Where it came from |
| ------ | ---------- | ------------------ |
| 1M | Orders on an ordinary day | Given. A large but not planetary retailer. |
| 5M | Orders on the worst day of the year | Black Friday runs 5× a normal day. |
| ~12/s | Average order rate | 1M ÷ 86,400. |
| ~170/s | Peak order rate | Peak hour holds ~12% of the peak day: 600k ÷ 3,600. |
| 200/s | What we'll actually design for | 170 plus headroom, because the number you design for should not be the number you predicted. |
| ~120:1 | Browse-to-buy ratio | People look far more than they buy. This is the number that decides the read architecture. |
| ~25,000/s | Peak read requests | 200 × 120 = 24,000, rounded up. Two orders of magnitude above the write path. |
| ~2.5 KB | One order plus its line items | ~1.5 KB order row, ~3 items at ~300 B. |
| ~900 GB/yr | Raw order data | 365M orders × 2.5 KB. |
| ~2 TB/yr | Order data with indexes | Indexes routinely double a transactional table. Budget for them. |
| 7 years | Retention | Tax law, not product. Hot for 18 months, archived after. |

</Table>

### The number that ends the sharding argument

<Note type="key" label="Read this line twice">

**200 writes per second is not a lot.** A single well-tuned Postgres or MySQL server on decent hardware handles 1,000–10,000 single-row writes per second. We're an order of magnitude below what one machine can do. Everything hard about this design is on the *read* path and the *failure* path. Skip the arithmetic and you'll spend three months sharding a database that was never the problem.

</Note>

### How long may "Place order" take?

One more number nobody asks for and everyone needs: the **latency budget**, how long the customer may wait after pressing the button. Pick a target and defend it: say 99% of checkouts finish within 800 ms (written **p99 under 800 ms**) and half within 300 ms (**p50 under 300 ms**).

That figure is what makes the rest of this guide decidable. From here on, "can we do this while the customer waits?" has an arithmetic answer instead of an opinion.

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="requirements" title="2. Deciding what we're not building">

Halfway through the design review someone asks, "what about fraud scoring? Multi-currency? Returns?" If you never said what's out, every question becomes a new requirement and the design never finishes.

So split the requirements on purpose. **Functional requirements** are what the system does. **Non-functional requirements** are how well it must do it: speed, safety, survival. The second list is what makes the design hard, and it's what interviewers care about.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Functional">
    View a cart. Place an order. Take payment. Confirm to the customer. Reserve inventory so we don't sell the same item twice. Show order status and history. Let the customer cancel while it's still cancellable.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Non-functional">
    p99 under 800 ms on checkout. Never charge without an order. Never lose an order we confirmed. Survive a payment provider being slow. Survive the loss of one availability zone (an isolated data centre, or group of them, within a cloud region). Absorb 5× traffic on a known date.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Explicitly out of scope">
    Search and recommendations. Pricing and promotions engines. Returns and refunds beyond the compensating action. Fraud scoring beyond "there is a service and we call it." Multi-currency and tax calculation. Anything to do with the warehouse past handing it a message.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Assumptions stated out loud">
    One region to start. Payment is an external provider, not something we build. Catalogue and pricing come from an existing service. Traffic peaks are known in advance, not surprises.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

Scoping out loud isn't throat-clearing. It's the difference between a design you can defend and one that grows a requirement every time someone asks a question. The out-of-scope list is the most useful list on the page.

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- Turn the brief into numbers before choosing any tool: orders per second at peak, reads per order, data per year, and a latency budget.
- Here the writes (200/s) fit on one database with room to spare. The reads (25,000/s) and the failures are the real problems.
- Write down what's out of scope. It stops the design from growing with every question.

</Note>

</Collapse>
