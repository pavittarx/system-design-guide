<Recap>

**Push makes servers remember.** Once the server holds a connection per user, it can't forget them between requests. That breaks the easy kind of load balancing, deploys and capacity planning all at once.

**Climb the ladder only as far as you must.** Polling for rare updates, SSE when only the server streams, WebSockets when the browser streams too. SSE is the most under-used option by a wide margin.

**Connections are cheap; what you attach to them isn't.** 50 KB each is 5 GB at 100,000 connections. 500 KB each is 50 GB. Keep per-connection state small and send queues bounded.

**Something must know which server the user is on.** Write it in a registry, broadcast to every gateway, or split messages into topics gateways subscribe to. The last one is what scales.

**Copy posts into inboxes for normal users; fetch them at read time for celebrities.** 100 million inbox writes for one post is not a design, and most of those inboxes are never opened.

**A signal costs how often it changes times how many people get it.** Send presence only to viewers, batch it, wait before marking people offline, and never store it.

**Sequence numbers do the heavy lifting.** They let a reconnecting client get exactly what it missed, make resuming cheap, catch duplicates, and let you disconnect slow clients safely.

**The reconnect is the load test.** Random backoff on the client, cheap resume, and a cap on new connections at the gateway, all shipped before the incident.

**Give every send queue a limit.** Drop, combine, or disconnect. Unlimited buffering means one slow client can kill a server full of healthy ones.

**Heartbeat everything.** Timeouts you never set will close quiet connections, and TCP won't tell you.

</Recap>
