<Collapse id="sharding" title="1. Cutting the data into pieces">

Replicas gave you more machines, but each one still holds *all* the data, and every write still goes through one primary. When the data no longer fits comfortably on one machine, or the writes outrun it, copying doesn't help. You have to cut the data into pieces and put different pieces in different places.

<DiagramDatabaseScaling2 />

### Two ways to cut: by table or by row

Cutting data into pieces is called **partitioning**, and there are two kinds that people often mix up:

- **By table: vertical partitioning.** Move whole groups of tables (or rarely used wide columns) to separate databases: billing tables on one, content tables on another. It's the gentlest way to spread load across machines, because each database still holds complete tables and queries inside one group work as before. Figma started here.
- **By row: horizontal partitioning.** Keep the same columns, but divide the rows by a key: by month, say, or by a hash of the ID. Inside one database engine this is simply called "partitioning". The app still queries one logical table, and the engine skips the pieces a query doesn't need.

Horizontal partitioning on one server helps with table size, bloated indexes and cleanup: drop last year's partition instantly instead of running a giant `DELETE`. But it's still one machine's CPU and disk. Once rows have to spread across *separate machines*, horizontal partitioning becomes sharding.

### Sharding: rows spread across machines

<Note type="wild" label="◈ In the wild · Figma">

In 2020 Figma ran a single Postgres instance on AWS's largest box. It first moved tables like Files and Organizations onto separate databases (vertical partitioning), reaching about a dozen by late 2022. When some tables hit terabytes and billions of rows, it built horizontal sharding in-house: several shard keys, related tables grouped so joins stay on one shard, and a routing service called DBProxy. [(Figma Engineering)](https://www.figma.com/blog/how-figmas-databases-team-lived-to-tell-the-scale/)

</Note>

**Sharding** is horizontal partitioning across independent database servers. Each **shard** holds a slice of the rows on its own hardware, so writes, storage and disk throughput all grow as you add machines. It's the most powerful move on the ladder, and by far the most complex.

### The shard key decides everything

To shard, you pick a **shard key**, the column that decides where a row lives (user ID, org ID, channel ID…), and a rule that maps each key to a shard. Every later decision follows from it, because it decides how evenly load spreads and which queries can stay on one machine.

Take user 1042 and three ways to place it:

- **Range:** users 1 to 500,000 live on shard A, the next 500,000 on shard B. User 1042 is on A.
- **Hash:** compute a hash of 1042 and take the remainder after dividing by the shard count. With 16 shards, the answer is a number from 0 to 15.
- **Directory:** look up 1042 in a separate table that says which shard it's on.

<Table>

| Strategy | How it decides | Strength | Weakness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Range** | Contiguous key ranges (A–M, N–Z; or by date) | Fast range scans; easy to reason about | Hot spots: recent dates or popular ranges overload one shard |
| **Hash** | Hash the key, remainder by shard count | Even spread | Range queries hit every shard; changing the shard count moves almost every row |
| **Directory** | A lookup table maps key → shard | Can place and move individual keys | The lookup service is a new dependency and potential bottleneck |

</Table>

Why changing the shard count moves almost everything under hashing: the remainder after dividing by 16 and the remainder after dividing by 17 are different for almost every key, so almost every row now belongs somewhere else.

<Note type="wild" label="◈ In the wild · Discord">

Discord partitions messages by channel and buckets them by time, keeping one conversation's history together while spreading total load across the cluster. Getting there took two migrations: MongoDB to Cassandra, then Cassandra to ScyllaDB, a C++ Cassandra-compatible store without a garbage collector, to tame tail latency and operations. The key matches how people read: one channel at a time. [(Discord Engineering)](https://discord.com/blog/how-discord-stores-trillions-of-messages)

</Note>

### The four taxes you pay

- **Hot keys.** If one tenant, channel or celebrity dominates traffic, their shard melts while the others idle. Hashing spreads *keys* evenly, but a single hot key still lands on one shard. (This is the database twin of the [cache hot-key problem](/guides/understanding-caching/).)
- **Queries across shards.** Anything that spans shards must ask each one and merge the answers in your application, including joins.
- **Transactions across shards.** An all-or-nothing change across two shards needs two-phase commit or a saga. Most teams avoid it by choosing a key that keeps related data together; [Consistency & CAP](/guides/consistency-cap/) covers what it costs when you can't.
- **Resharding.** Adding shards means moving live data without downtime.

<Note label="The pro move: many small shards on a few big machines">

Create many *logical* shards up front and pack them onto a few *physical* machines. To grow, move whole logical shards to new machines. Why it works: the rule from key to logical shard never changes, so nothing has to be re-hashed; only the map from logical shard to machine does. Notion did exactly this when it sharded Postgres: 480 logical shards (each a Postgres schema) spread across 32 physical databases. [(Notion Engineering)](https://www.notion.com/blog/sharding-postgres-at-notion)

</Note>

### Which shard has ada@example.com?

Users are sharded by `user_id`, and someone logs in with an email address. The email doesn't tell you the shard. "Add an index on `email`" now means one of two very different things:

- **Each shard indexes its own rows: a local index.** Cheap and always correct, because the index updates in the same transaction as the write. But any lookup that isn't by shard key has to ask every shard: 16 shards, 16 queries, as slow as the slowest. Fine until login-by-email gets popular and one lookup pattern eats the cluster.
- **One separate index maps email → shard: a global index.** One lookup, no asking around. But it lives on other machines, so every insert is now a write to two places: update the index later and accept that it's briefly wrong, or pay for two-phase commit on every write. DynamoDB makes this trade openly: its global secondary indexes are eventually consistent by design.

A global index is not a feature you switch on. It's a distributed system you now run.

Sharding also assumes app servers keep no per-user state in memory, so any app server can talk to any shard. If yours still do, fix that first; see [Load Balancing](/guides/load-balancing/).

### The bills that arrive later

Sharding gets planned as a one-off engineering cost. It's mostly recurring costs, paid by people who weren't in the room:

- **Data transfer.** Shards in different availability zones mean cross-zone traffic billed per gigabyte, growing with every query that asks all shards.
- **Backups multiply.** 16 shards is 16 backup jobs, and point-in-time recovery has to be coordinated across all of them. Your recovery time is the slowest shard's.
- **Every migration becomes N migrations,** and it can end up half-applied, so [expand and contract](/guides/database-scaling/correct/#migrations) stops being good practice and becomes mandatory.
- **Resharding is a project:** a plan, a backfill, a dual-write period, a cutover and a rollback story. Weeks, with a named owner.
- **More things can page you.** One database with a replica is two; 16 shards with replicas is 32.

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="worked-shard" title="2. Worked example: picking a shard key, as arithmetic">

Shard-key choice is usually taught as a list of strategies. It's easier to get right as arithmetic plus a short list of queries you refuse to make slow. Take a Slack-shaped product: **workspaces** contain **channels**, channels contain **messages**, and everything a user does happens inside one workspace at a time.

### Step 1: work out why you're sharding at all

```trace
**$ the shape of the data**
  workspaces .......................... 250,000
  messages / day ...................... 30,000,000
  avg row + index overhead ............ 400 bytes
  data added per day .................. 12 GB
  retention ........................... 2 years (730 days)

**$ therefore**
  total rows .......... 30M × 730 ..... 21,900,000,000
  total size .......... 21.9B × 400B .. ~8.8 TB
  comfortable data per shard .......... ~1.5 TB
  minimum shards ...... 8.8 / 1.5 ..... 5.9  →  **6**
  provisioned (headroom, power of two)  **16**
  logical shards ...................... 1,024  (64 per machine)

**$ write rate**
  average ............. 30M / 86,400 .. ~350 writes/s
  peak (4× average) ................... ~1,400 writes/s
  per shard at 16 shards ..............   ~90 writes/s
```

Read that last line again. Ninety writes a second is *nothing*; a laptop does it. You're not sharding for write speed. You're sharding because 8.8 TB of hot data and its indexes doesn't fit comfortably on one machine, and because a 22-billion-row table turns every backup and schema change into a negotiation. Knowing *which* limit forces your hand changes what a good shard key looks like.

### Step 2: test each candidate against the queries people actually run

For each key, ask which queries stay on one shard, and what the spread of data actually looks like.

<Table>

| Shard key | What stays on one shard | What breaks | Verdict |
| --------- | ----------------------- | ----------- | ------- |
| **message_id** (hashed) | Fetching one message by ID. That's the whole list. | "Last 50 messages in this channel", the most common query in the product, asks all 16 shards and throws away almost everything. | <Pill tone="bad">no</Pill> Perfect spread, useless for how people read |
| **time range** | Recent history lands on the newest shard; dropping old data is instant. | 100% of writes go to whichever shard holds "now": a 16-machine cluster with one machine working. | <Pill tone="bad">no</Pill> Great for archiving, fatal as a shard key |
| **user_id** | "Everything I ever posted." Writes spread beautifully. | A channel's messages come from hundreds of users, so showing one channel asks all 16 shards. | <Pill tone="bad">no</Pill> Spread isn't the problem you have |
| **channel_id** | A channel's history is one shard, one index scan. | Anything workspace-wide asks every shard: unread badges, "mark all read", workspace search, membership checks. | <Pill tone="mid">close</Pill> Right instinct, one level too fine |
| **workspace_id** | Channels, memberships, messages, unread state and search for a workspace sit on one machine, and ordinary transactions and joins *within* a workspace keep working. | A few huge workspaces and many tiny ones, and cross-workspace queries have nowhere to go. | <Pill tone="good">yes</Pill> How people use the product decides it |

</Table>

### Step 3: what workspace_id costs you, stated honestly

- **One workspace can outgrow a shard.** The median workspace has a dozen people; your largest has 200,000 seats writing 2 million messages a day. That's 800 MB a day, about 290 GB a year, and *6.7% of all writes on one shard* when an even split would be 6.25%: your biggest customer alone outweighs an average shard. Plan before you need it: a directory to pin the biggest customers to dedicated shards, and a second-level key (`channel_id` within the workspace) for the day one customer stops fitting. Slack hit exactly this. It began with all of a workspace's data on one shard; as its largest customers reached the biggest hardware available, it moved to Vitess, partly so message data could be sharded by channel instead. [(Slack Engineering)](https://slack.engineering/scaling-datastores-at-slack-with-vitess/)
- **Cross-workspace queries have to ask every shard.** Global admin search, analytics and compliance exports touch every shard and wait for the slowest. Don't make that fast on the shards; stop asking the shards. Feed a search index or data warehouse from a stream of every change (change-data-capture).
- **Rebalancing becomes a permanent job.** Workspaces grow unevenly, so you'll move logical shards between machines periodically, forever. It's the right trade for keeping the common path on one shard, but someone has to own it.

The rule underneath the arithmetic: pick the shard key that matches the boundary your product already draws around data. If almost every request happens inside one workspace, tenant or account, that's your shard key. It turns "distributed database" back into "a lot of small ordinary databases" for the 99% of queries that stay inside the boundary.

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="beyond" title="3. Or buy it: distributed SQL, NoSQL and the CAP dial">

Building sharding yourself means writing the routing, the resharding tooling and the cross-shard queries, then running them forever. The alternative is an engine built to spread data across machines from the start:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Distributed SQL">
    Spanner, CockroachDB, TiDB, YugabyteDB: SQL and transactions, with data automatically sharded and replicated. Scale across machines without writing your own shard router.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Sharding middleware">
    Vitess (born at YouTube, for MySQL) and Citus (for Postgres) add sharding on top of engines you already know.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Wide-column NoSQL">
    Cassandra, ScyllaDB, DynamoDB, Bigtable: built for huge write volume, at the cost of flexible queries and, often, strong consistency.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The trade-off">
    They solve distribution for you, but impose their own data model and consistency rules. You trade freedom and familiarity for scale that "just works".
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

Whichever you pick, once data lives on more than one machine you'll eventually face this: the network between two machines breaks, and each can still hear from users but not from the other. This is a **network partition**, and the **CAP theorem** says that during one you must choose. Either refuse some requests so nobody reads stale data (**consistency**: every read sees the latest write), or keep answering everyone and accept that some answers are out of date (**availability**: every request gets a response).

In practice it's a dial you set per feature: strong consistency for money, inventory and logins; eventual consistency for feeds and counts. It's the same call you made with cache TTLs and replication lag. [Consistency & CAP](/guides/consistency-cap/) takes it apart properly.

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- Shard only when one machine can't hold the data or absorb the writes, and work out which of the two it is.
- Choose the shard key from the boundary your product already draws, so most queries stay on one shard.
- Sharding's costs are permanent: hot keys, cross-shard queries, N backups, N migrations, resharding as a project.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="You shard by user_id. One customer's shared service account generates most of their API traffic and melts the shard it lands on. What should you have done?" options={["Planned for hot keys by pinning or splitting them, and sharded on the customer (tenant) boundary instead of user_id", "Used a better hash function to spread the keys", "Added read replicas to that shard"]} answer={0}>

Hashing spreads *keys* evenly, not *load per key*: one hot key always lands on exactly one shard, however good the hash. Hot keys need their own mechanism: a directory entry that pins the key to a dedicated shard, a second-level key to split it, or caching and combining requests in front.

And underneath: in a product where each request belongs to one customer, `user_id` scatters every customer across every shard, so their queries were already asking all shards. The customer boundary was the shard key.

</Quiz>
