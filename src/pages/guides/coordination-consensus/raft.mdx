<Collapse id="raft" title="1. How Raft picks one leader and commits a write">

You run three nodes, and the leader crashes. The other two must each decide, without a reliable way to talk, whether to take over. If neither does, you're stuck. If both do, you have two leaders, which is worse. An algorithm that gets a group of machines to make one decision anyway is called a **consensus algorithm**.

Paxos is the famous one; **Raft** is the one people actually implement, because it was designed to be understandable. Every managed coordination service you'll use runs something close to it.

### Three roles and a counter

Every node is a **follower**, a **candidate** (asking to become leader) or the **leader**. Time is divided into numbered **terms**: a counter that only goes up, with at most one leader per term. It's how an out-of-date leader gets caught.

### How an election works

<Stack>
  <Layer idx="1">**Everyone starts as a follower.** Followers only respond to messages. Each one holds an election timeout picked at random, typically 150–300 ms.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="2">**The leader sends heartbeats.** While heartbeats keep arriving, followers reset their timers and stay followers.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="3">**Silence starts a candidacy.** A follower whose timer runs out adds one to the term, votes for itself, and asks every other node for a vote.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="4">**One vote per node per term.** A node votes for a candidate only if the candidate's log is at least as up to date as its own. This stops a node that missed writes from becoming leader.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="5">**A majority wins.** The candidate with votes from a majority of the cluster becomes leader and immediately starts sending heartbeats, which stops anyone else from starting an election.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="6">**A tie just tries again.** If nobody gets a majority, the term ends with no leader. Timers run out again at different random times, and the next round almost always succeeds.</Layer>
</Stack>

### Why the timeouts are random

Step 6 needs a picture. Five nodes; the leader has just crashed. Suppose two followers had exactly the same timeout:

That's a **split vote**, and with identical timers it could repeat every round. With random timeouts, one node's timer almost certainly fires first next round, and it collects its votes before anyone else wakes up.

### How a write gets committed

Once elected, the leader handles every write as an entry added to the end of a **log**, an ordered list of changes:

<Stack>
  <Layer idx="1">**The leader appends the entry** to its own log.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="2">**It sends the entry to every follower** and waits for replies.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="3">**When a majority has stored it, the entry is committed.** It's now permanent whatever fails next, because any future leader must have it in order to win an election.</Layer>
  <Layer idx="4">**Only then does the leader apply the change** and answer the client.</Layer>
</Stack>

One detail from the Raft paper: a leader counts copies this way only for entries from *its own* term; older entries commit along with them.

<Note type="key" label="The two guarantees that make it work">

**Election restriction:** each voter refuses candidates whose log is behind its own, and a winner needs a majority of votes. So the winner's log is at least as current as every node in some majority, which means it has every committed entry.

**Term monotonicity:** every message carries its sender's term. A node rejects messages from an older term, and a leader or candidate that sees a newer term steps down immediately. This is how a cut-off ex-leader learns it's been replaced, the moment it can talk to anyone again.

</Note>

### The leader that didn't know it was replaced

That second guarantee is what prevents two leaders inside the cluster. Five nodes; the leader, in term 3, gets cut off with one follower, while the other three can still talk:

<Race a="Old leader (term 3, minority side)" b="Majority side (3 nodes)" result="Result: only one side could ever commit, so there is no conflict to merge" tone="good" steps={[
  { b: "Heartbeats stop. One node times out and wins term 4 with 3 votes" },
  { a: "Still believes it leads. Accepts a client write" },

The old leader can never commit anything, because committing needs a majority it doesn't have. It's a leader with no power, which is exactly the right way to fail.

<Collapse id="quorum" title="2. Why a majority, and why an odd number">

Two data centres, two nodes each. The link between them breaks, and each side sees the other go quiet. Which side carries on? If both do, you get GitHub's split brain. Raft's answer is the rule you just saw: only a group holding a **majority** may decide anything.

### The majority rule

$$
\text{majority of } n \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \right\rfloor + 1
$$

(The ⌊ ⌋ brackets mean "round down".) The group of nodes whose agreement counts is called a **quorum**, and here it's a majority.

Why this works: two majorities of the same cluster must share at least one node. Take five nodes, A to E. If A, B and C agreed on one thing and C, D and E later decided something else, C was in both groups, so the second decision was made by a group that already knew about the first. Any two decisions share a witness, so they can't contradict each other. And in the 2+2 split above, neither side has 3 of 4, so neither side carries on.

### What each size buys you

<Table numeric>

| Nodes | Majority | Failures tolerated | Verdict |
| ----- | -------- | ------------------ | ------- |
| 1 | 1 | 0 | Not a cluster |
| 2 | 2 | **0** | Worse than 1: twice the machines that can fail, no tolerance |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | The sensible minimum |
| 4 | 3 | **1** | Same tolerance as 3, more cost, slower writes |
| 5 | 3 | 2 | The standard for anything important |
| 7 | 4 | 3 | Rarely worth the latency |

</Table>

Hence the odd-number rule: **an even-sized cluster gives you nothing the odd size below it doesn't.** Four nodes survive as many failures as three, while needing one more acknowledgement per write. Going from 3 to 5 is a real upgrade. Going from 3 to 4 is paying money to get slower.

### Bigger clusters are slower

A write is as slow as the *slowest node in the fastest majority*: with five nodes you wait for the third-fastest, with seven the fourth-fastest. Consensus clusters get slower as they grow, the opposite of stateless services in [Load Balancing](/guides/load-balancing/).

### Spreading across regions costs every write

Put five etcd nodes on three continents and every write needs a cross-ocean round trip to reach a majority: 150 ms or more, per write, forever. So keep the consensus group in one region, or a few nearby availability zones, and let other regions receive its decisions asynchronously. When you truly need agreement across the globe, you're in the territory of Spanner and its atomic clocks, covered in [Consistency & the CAP Theorem](/guides/consistency-cap/).

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- Raft elects a leader by majority vote within a numbered term; random timeouts keep ties rare, and only a node with every committed write can win.
- A write is committed once a majority has stored it. A leader cut off from the majority can't commit anything, and steps down when it sees a newer term.
- Use 3 or 5 nodes, close together. Any two majorities overlap, which is why consensus is safe; even sizes add cost and latency without adding tolerance.

[Part 2](/guides/coordination-consensus/safety/) is about what happens outside the cluster, where systems act on what it told them.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="Why are even-numbered cluster sizes (like 4 nodes) almost always a bad design?" options={["A 4-node cluster tolerates the same single failure as 3 nodes, but every write waits for one more acknowledgement", "Raft cannot elect a leader when the number of voters is even", "An even cluster can split into two equal halves that each elect their own leader"]} answer={0}>

A 4-node cluster needs 3 nodes to agree (4 ÷ 2 + 1 = 3), so it survives only 1 failure (4 − 3 = 1). A 3-node cluster needs 2, so it also survives 1. The fourth node adds no tolerance, but it adds hardware cost, slower writes (waiting for 3 acknowledgements instead of 2), and another machine that can fail.

An even cluster can split into two equal halves, but neither half has a majority, so neither elects a leader. That's safe, just unavailable.

</Quiz>
