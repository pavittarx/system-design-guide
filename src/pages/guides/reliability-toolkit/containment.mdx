<Collapse id="breakers" title="1. Stop calling what is down: circuit breakers">

The recommendations service is completely down. Every call to it waits out its 2-second timeout and then fails. At 400 requests a second, you spend 800 thread-seconds every second learning something you already knew, and the struggling service gets hammered with requests it can't answer while it's trying to restart.

A **circuit breaker**, named after the one in your house, fixes this. It watches calls to a dependency, and after it sees enough failures it *trips*: for a while it **fails instantly**, without even attempting the call. It moves through three states:

<Stack>
  <Layer idx="✓" title="Closed" speed="healthy">

  Calls flow normally. Failures are counted.

  </Layer>
  <Arrow>↓ too many failures ↓</Arrow>
  <Layer idx="✕" title="Open" speed="tripped">

  Stop calling entirely. Fail fast, return a fallback. Give the sick service room to recover.

  </Layer>
  <Arrow>↓ after a cooldown ↓</Arrow>
  <Layer idx="?" title="Half-open" speed="probing">

  Let a trickle of test calls through. Succeed → close. Fail → open again.

  </Layer>
</Stack>

Why this works: an open breaker does two kind things at once. It protects *you*, because no threads are wasted on calls that are doomed. And it protects *them*, because the struggling service gets breathing room instead of a beating. It's the single most important pattern for stopping cascades.

<Note type="wild" label="◈ In the wild · Netflix Hystrix">

Netflix built and open-sourced [Hystrix](https://github.com/Netflix/Hystrix/wiki), the library that did more than anything else to popularise the circuit-breaker pattern in microservices. It wraps each call to a dependency, trips open once that dependency's error rate crosses a threshold, and then sheds load fast instead of piling more attempts onto a service that's already struggling. Netflix has since retired Hystrix in favour of newer libraries like resilience4j, but the pattern it spread is now everywhere.

</Note>

<Collapse id="bulkheads" title="2. Wall off the damage: bulkheads and load shedding">

### One slow feature shouldn't take every thread

Your server has one pool of 200 threads shared by every endpoint. Recommendations slows down and its calls hold threads for seconds each. Soon all 200 are waiting on it, and checkout, login and the health check have nothing left to run on.

A **bulkhead**, named after a ship's watertight compartments, stops a flood in one compartment sinking the whole ship. Give calls to recommendations their own small pool, say 20 threads. They can exhaust *that* pool, but they can't take threads from checkout. One feature browns out; the ship stays afloat.

### Too much work: say no quickly

A different problem: nothing is broken, but three times the usual traffic arrives. Try to serve everyone and every request gets slow, queues grow, and requests time out before you even look at them. Everyone is served badly.

**Load shedding** means deliberately rejecting some requests at the door, with a quick "try again in a moment", so that the rest are served well. It's better to serve *most* requests well than *all* requests badly. Counterintuitive but vital: sometimes saying "no" fast is the most reliable thing you can do. It's a close relative of the health checks and connection draining in the [load balancing guide](/guides/load-balancing/): same idea, applied at the door rather than at the pool.

<Lab
  title="Build a Concurrency-Limiting Middleware"
  tip="Add this middleware benchmarking demo to your portfolio to prove you understand overload protection."
>

Build this mini-project to implement active request limits and load shedding (protecting server thread/memory pool):

- **The Slow Endpoint:** Write an HTTP server in Go or Node.js with a CPU-bound slow route `/hash` that computes a bcrypt password hash (simulated 200ms latency).

- **Concurrency-Limiting Gate:** Implement a middleware that tracks active in-flight requests using a counter. Set a hard limit of **10 concurrent requests**.

- **Load Shedding:** If a request arrives and the counter is at 10, reject the request immediately with an HTTP status code `503 Service Unavailable` or `429 Too Many Requests`.

- **Run the Load Test:** Use `k6` to hit the route with **50 concurrent virtual users**. Observe that while some requests get rejected, the p99 latency for successfully served requests remains flat around 200ms. Remove the limiter, run the test again, and watch p99 climb as requests queue up behind each other.

</Lab>

<Collapse id="degrade" title="3. Lose a feature, not the page: graceful degradation">

The recommendations engine is down. Should the product page show an error? Of course not: show a generic "popular items" list, or nothing, and *still let people check out*. The search ranking service died? Fall back to plain results. When a non-essential dependency fails, drop the feature and keep the core. This is called **graceful degradation**. Users forgive a missing sidebar; they don't forgive a white screen.

<Note type="key" label="The mindset">

Ask of every feature: "if this dependency vanishes, what's the least-bad thing we can show?" Design that fallback *on purpose*, before the incident. A system that degrades gracefully feels far more reliable than one that's either perfect or dead.

</Note>

Notice what you're doing when you serve stale prices from a cache because the pricing service is down: you're choosing to be available over being correct, one feature at a time. That's the choice from the [consistency and CAP guide](/guides/consistency-cap/), made concrete. Graceful degradation is the "stay available" branch, chosen deliberately and limited to the parts of the product where slightly wrong beats not there. Checkout totals get the other answer.

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- A circuit breaker stops calling a dependency that is clearly failing, which saves your threads and gives it room to recover.
- A bulkhead gives each dependency its own limited pool, so one slow feature can't take every thread. Load shedding rejects excess work quickly when there's simply too much.
- Decide each feature's fallback before the incident; a missing sidebar beats a white screen.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="check" title="Check your understanding">

<Quiz q="There was clearly a dependency outage, but your circuit breaker never tripped. What is the most likely reason?" options={["The cooldown was too short, so the breaker closed again before anyone noticed", "It was a gray failure, slow or 5%-failing responses, and the breaker only counted explicit errors against a high threshold", "Half-open probes hit a healthy instance and kept the circuit closed"]} answer={1}>

Most likely it was a gray failure — a 5% error rate, or responses that were slow but successful — and a breaker watching for a 50% error rate on explicit errors will never see it. That's the fix too: count timeouts and slow responses as failures, not just non-2xx.

The other common causes are a threshold or window set too loose to trigger inside the incident's duration, and a breaker scoped per-instance across a large fleet, where each instance sees too few failures individually to cross its own threshold even though the aggregate picture is obvious. Breakers fail silently in the wrong direction, which is why "did it trip?" belongs on the postmortem checklist.

</Quiz>
