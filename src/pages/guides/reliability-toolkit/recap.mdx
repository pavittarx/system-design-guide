<Recap>

- **Redundancy** answers "a machine died". Have two of everything critical, and it's useless until you've actually switched over to the spare.

- **Timeout** answers "it's slow". It caps how long you wait, and so caps how many requests can pile up waiting: at most `throughput × timeout`.

- **Retry with backoff and jitter** answers "it blipped". It spreads retries out in time; it doesn't reduce how many there are.

- **Retry budget** answers "everything blipped at once". It caps retries at a small share of live traffic, which is the half that causes outages.

- **Circuit breaker** answers "it's down". It stops paying for calls you know will fail and gives the sick service room. It does nothing about overload.

- **Bulkhead** answers "one thing is eating everything". It's the only tool that gives a hard limit, not just a smaller number.

- **Load shedding** answers "there's more work than machine". It rejects the excess fast. It does nothing about a slow dependency.

- **Graceful degradation** answers "this part is optional". Decide each feature's least-bad fallback before the incident.

- **Deadline propagation** passes the time left down the call chain, so every hop's timeout fits inside the caller's instead of contradicting it.

- **Chaos engineering** breaks things on purpose. It's the only evidence that any of the above works.

- **Metastable failure** is the outage that outlives its cause: retries and cold caches keep it going. Remove load to escape; waiting doesn't work.

</Recap>

And the numbers worth carrying in your head:

<Table numeric>

| Number | What it means |
| ------ | ------------- |
| 99% | 3.65 days of downtime a year — about 7.3 hours a month. "Two nines" sounds respectable and is nearly a full day per quarter. |
| 99.9% | 8.76 hours a year — about 43.8 minutes a month. The default target most teams claim without checking. |
| 99.95% | 4.38 hours a year — about 21.9 minutes a month. A common contractual middle ground. |
| 99.99% | 52.6 minutes a year — about 4.4 minutes a month. Less than one bad deploy's worth. Requires automated recovery; humans are too slow to be in the loop. |
| 0.999<sup>10</sup> ≈ 99.0% | Ten hard dependencies at three nines each. Your ceiling is the product, and it is much worse than any input. |
| p99 × 1.5–2 | *Starting point* for a timeout. Measure your p99 first, set it there, then check what fraction of real traffic that number would have killed last week. |
| 2 retries | *Starting point* for a retry cap — three attempts total, at exactly one layer, only on idempotent operations. |
| ≈10% | *Starting point* for a retry budget: retries capped at roughly a tenth of live traffic, with the bucket refilled by successes. |
| ≈50% / 20 reqs | *Starting point* for a circuit breaker: trip at about a 50% error rate, but only once the window holds at least ~20 requests, so a quiet endpoint can't trip on two unlucky calls. |
| 1–5 probes | *Starting point* for half-open: a trickle, jittered across instances, never the whole fleet resuming at once. |
| concurrency<br />= rps × latency | Little's Law. Sizes your pools, sizes your bulkheads, and explains every cascade you will ever debug. |

</Table>

<Note label="Read that table correctly">

The availability rows are arithmetic — they're true regardless of what you're building. Everything below them is a **starting point to measure against, not a benchmark to hit.** A batch job and a payment API have no business sharing a timeout, and anyone who quotes you a universally correct retry count is quoting you a number they didn't measure.

</Note>
