<ColdOpen tag="◇ the domino that ate the datacenter">

The recommendations service gets slow. Not down, just slow. The product page calls it and waits. Each waiting request holds a thread, and threads pile up. The web servers run out of threads, so *they* get slow. The load balancer marks them unhealthy and moves their traffic onto the survivors, which now drown too. In ninety seconds, a hiccup in a service nobody would call critical has taken down the whole site. Nothing "broke". It *cascaded*. This guide is the set of tools that stop the dominoes.

</ColdOpen>

<Collapse id="truth" title="1. Something is always broken, so plan to fail small">

Run one server and a disk failure is a rare event. Run thousands and it's a normal Tuesday: somewhere a disk is dying, a network link is flapping, a deploy is misbehaving, a dependency is having a bad day. With enough machines, the rare becomes the constant, and you can't engineer that away.

So the goal changes from "don't fail" to **"fail small, keep the failure contained, and keep serving what you still can."** This is called **resilience**, and it's a set of specific, learnable moves. The rest of this guide is those moves.

<Note type="key" label="Two numbers worth knowing">

**Availability** is the share of time a system works, often quoted in "nines". "Three nines" (99.9%) allows ~8.76 hours of downtime a year (about 8h 46m); "four nines" (99.99%) allows ~52 minutes; "five nines" about 5 minutes. Each extra nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more effort, which is why "how many nines do we actually need?" is a business question, not just an engineering one.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="redundancy" title="2. Never have just one, and prove the spare works">

Your only database server's disk dies at 2 a.m. Everything that needs the database is down until someone restores it. The fix is the foundation of everything else: never have exactly one of anything critical. Two servers, a replicated database, several availability zones. When one dies, another takes over. This is called **failover**.

The [load balancing guide](/guides/load-balancing/) covered the mechanism and the [database scaling guide](/guides/database-scaling/) covered the replica side. The principle here is broader: *a single point of failure is a scheduled outage whose date you haven't been told yet.*

The catch is that failover itself must be tested. A spare you have never actually switched to isn't a backup, it's a hope. Plenty of outages are made worse by a standby that turned out to be misconfigured, out of date, or, memorably, also on fire.

<Note type="wild" label="◈ In the wild · AWS S3 2017 Outage">

On 28 February 2017, while debugging a slow S3 billing system, an engineer ran a routine command with one input mistyped. It removed far more servers than intended, including ones S3's index subsystem needed in us-east-1. S3 was down for hours, breaking thousands of sites and AWS services that hard-depended on it.

</Note>

That last phrase is the lesson. When services depend on each other with no fallback, their availabilities multiply: if you need ten services that each work 99.9% of the time, you work at most about 99.0% of the time. [Part 3](/guides/reliability-toolkit/practice/#arithmetic) does that arithmetic.

<Note type="key" label="What to remember">

- At scale something is always failing. Aim to fail small and contained, not to never fail.
- Have at least two of anything critical, and actually fail over to the spare regularly; an untested spare is a hope.
- Every hard dependency multiplies into your own availability. The toolkit in this guide is about breaking that chain.

</Note>

</Collapse>
