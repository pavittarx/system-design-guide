<ColdOpen tag="◇ the domino that ate the datacenter">

The recommendations service gets slow — not down, just slow. The product page calls it and waits. Threads pile up waiting. The web servers run out of threads, so *they* get slow. The load balancer marks them unhealthy and piles their traffic onto the survivors, which now also drown. In ninety seconds a minor hiccup in a service nobody would call critical has taken down the entire site. Nothing "broke." It *cascaded.* This guide is the set of tools that stop the dominoes.

</ColdOpen>

<Collapse id="truth" title="1. The uncomfortable truth">

At scale, something is always broken. A disk somewhere is dying, a network link is flapping, a deploy is misbehaving, a dependency is having a bad day. You cannot engineer failure away — with enough machines, the rare becomes the constant. So the goal shifts from "don't fail" to **"fail small, fail contained, and keep serving what you still can."** This is *resilience*, and it's a set of specific, learnable moves.

<Note type="key" label="Two numbers worth knowing">

**Availability** is often quoted in nines. "Three nines" (99.9%) is ~8.76 hours of downtime a year (about 8h 46m); "four nines" (99.99%) is ~52 minutes; "five nines" is ~5 minutes. Each extra nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more effort — which is why "how many nines do we actually need?" is a business question, not just an engineering one.

</Note>

</Collapse>

<Collapse id="redundancy" title="2. Redundancy & failover">

The foundation: never have exactly one of anything critical. Two servers, replicated databases, multiple availability zones. When one dies, another takes over — **failover**. The [load balancing guide](/guides/load-balancing/) covered the mechanism and the [database scaling guide](/guides/database-scaling/) covered the replica side of it; the principle here is broader: *a single point of failure is a scheduled outage you haven't been told the date of yet.*

The subtlety is that failover itself must be tested. A backup that has never been failed-over to is not a backup — it's a hope. Plenty of outages are made worse by a standby that turned out to be misconfigured, out of date, or, memorably, also on fire.

<Note type="wild" label="◈ In the wild · AWS S3 2017 Outage">

On February 28, 2017, an engineer running a debugging tool typed a command that deleted far more S3 indexing servers than intended in the us-east-1 region. S3 went down for hours, which broke thousands of downstream sites and other AWS services that hard-depended on it. It highlighted the blast-radius ceiling: when services compose as hard dependencies, their availability drops to the product of all inputs.

</Note>

</Collapse>
