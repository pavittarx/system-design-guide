<Recap>

- **"Consistency" means two different things.** In CAP it means the system behaves like one copy. In ACID it means your own rules (constraints) hold. Ask which one someone means before you design anything.

- **When the network splits, you either refuse or answer with possibly-old data.** That's the whole CAP choice, and it only applies during a split. A single server is trivially "CP", which shows how little the label tells you.

- **A partition is a timeout you configured.** Machines never see a split, only silence longer than a limit you set. Shorter limit: more partitions, resolved faster. Longer: fewer, each hurting longer. There's no zero.

- **The everyday choice is speed versus agreement.** PACELC's "else" branch runs on every request: wait for other copies (consistent) or answer now (fast). The partition branch runs a few minutes a year.

- **R + W > N means the read and write groups share a machine.** That's all. It isn't linearizable, doesn't make read-modify-write safe, and is switched off during a sloppy quorum.

- **Last-write-wins quietly throws writes away.** If clocks disagree by 300 ms, so does the idea of "last", and nothing reports the loss. Version vectors honestly detect concurrent writes; CRDTs make merging automatic; neither decides what a conflict *means* for your business.

- **Holding a lock is a fact about the past.** A paused process can wake up and write after its lock expired. Give each lock a fencing token and have the storage reject old ones.

- **A saga's undo is not a rollback.** Everyone saw the in-between state, and some actions can't be undone, so put irreversible steps last. Two-phase commit's real danger is rows stuck locked when the coordinator dies mid-way.

- **Read-your-writes is the bug users notice.** Send their reads to the primary for a while, or carry an LSN/GTID and wait for a replica to catch up to it.

- **Choose per kind of data, not per system.** Strong for money and inventory, session guarantees for a user's own content, eventual for counters.

</Recap>

And the numbers underneath all of it. These are **order-of-magnitude** figures — your network is not these numbers, but it is within a factor of two or three of them, and that's enough to do the arithmetic that matters.

<Table numeric>

| Rough number | What it is | What it costs you |
| ------------ | ---------- | ----------------- |
| ~0.5 ms | Round trip within a rack / one AZ | A three-node quorum here is essentially free. This is why the default single-region setup feels fast and teaches you nothing about the trade-off. |
| ~1–2 ms | Round trip cross-AZ, same region | A synchronous quorum across three availability zones costs a millisecond or two per write. That's cheap insurance against losing a data centre — take it. |
| ~60–90 ms | Round trip cross-continent (US east↔west, US↔EU) | One majority round trip. A synchronous three-region quorum write cannot complete faster than the RTT to the *second-closest* region. Full stop. |
| ~200 ms | Round trip to the far side of the planet (~40,000 km of fibre) | The floor for a globally synchronous write. Not a target to optimise toward — a wall. |
| ~200,000 km/s | Speed of light in fibre, about two-thirds of *c* | The constant generating every row above. It has not improved and will not. |
| 1–2 RTT | Round trips a commit actually needs | Raft/Paxos in steady state is roughly one majority RTT; a prepare-then-commit protocol is two. Multiply the rows above, don't divide them. |

</Table>

The point those numbers exist to make: **synchronous cross-region consensus has a latency floor set by physics, and no engineering gets under it.** If someone asks for global strong consistency with a p99 under 50 ms, they haven't asked for an optimisation — they've asked you to move Europe closer. The only real move is to shrink the set of data that needs global agreement until what's left can afford the round trip, and let everything else be regional, cached, or eventual.

</Collapse>
