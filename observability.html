Seeing Inside the Machine

Every guide before this one told you what to build. This one tells you how to know whether it's working — because the alternative is finding out from a customer on social media.

A companion to The Reliability Toolkit and Load Balancing.

◇ the outage nobody could see

Support tickets start arriving at 09:14. Checkout is broken, they say. You open the dashboard: request rate normal, error rate 0.02%, CPU flat, database healthy, every panel a calm green. You cannot reproduce it. For forty minutes you argue with the evidence, because the evidence says nothing is wrong. At 09:56 someone tries a purchase on a phone over cellular data and watches it hang for eleven seconds before succeeding — a success, counted as a success, averaged into a mean that never moved. The system wasn't lying to you. You had simply built instrumentation that could only see the failures you'd already thought of.

1. Green dashboards, broken system

There is a specific kind of professional misery that comes from staring at a wall of healthy graphs while customers tell you the product is unusable. It is worth understanding exactly how it happens, because it is not bad luck — it's a predictable consequence of how most instrumentation gets built.

Monitoring, as most teams practise it, means picking a set of things that might break and watching them. CPU. Memory. Disk. Error rate. Those are all real, and every one of them has caught a real outage. But the list is written in advance, by people reasoning about failures they can imagine. The failure that takes you down is, almost by definition, the one nobody put on the list.

The distinction worth keeping Monitoring answers questions you thought of in advance. Observability is the property of a system that lets you answer questions you didn't think of in advance, without shipping new code. The first is a set of dashboards. The second is a property of your data: enough dimensions, retained at enough granularity, that you can slice a problem in a direction you've never sliced it before and still get an answer.

The checkout outage above is the canonical shape. Every dashboard was an aggregate. Aggregates are lossy on purpose — that's what makes them cheap. And the loss is not random: aggregation systematically hides small populations. A failure affecting 2% of users disappears into a 0.02% error rate if it manifests as slowness rather than errors. A failure affecting one customer — who happens to be your largest — disappears entirely.

So the practical question is not "do we have monitoring." Everyone has monitoring. The question is: when something breaks in a way we did not anticipate, can we find it from the data we're already collecting? Most teams discover the answer is no at the worst possible time.

2. Three signals, three jobs

The industry has more or less converged on three kinds of telemetry. They are not three formats for the same information — they are three different trade-offs between cost, breadth, and detail, and using the wrong one for a job is where most of the waste lives.

SignalWhat it isCost shapeAnswers
MetricsNumbers aggregated over time, tagged with a few labelsCost grows with distinct label combinations, not with traffic"Is something wrong, and roughly where?"
LogsDiscrete events, one record per thing that happenedCost grows linearly with traffic"What exactly happened to this one request?"
TracesOne request's journey across every service it touchedCost grows with traffic × services, usually sampled"Where did the time go, and who called whom?"

The cost shapes are the important column and the one people skip. Metrics are cheap at any traffic volume because ten million requests and ten requests both increment the same counter — but they get expensive fast when you add labels, and we'll spend a whole section on why. Logs are the opposite: one label costs nothing, but ten million requests cost ten million records. Traces sit in between and are almost always sampled, which introduces its own class of problem.

A rule of thumb that holds up Metrics tell you that something is wrong. Traces tell you where. Logs tell you why. If you find yourself trying to answer "why" from a metric, you are about to add a label you will regret.

A fourth signal, profiles (continuous CPU and memory profiling in production), has become practical in the last few years and is worth knowing about — it answers "which line of code is burning the CPU" in the same way traces answer "which service is burning the latency." It's not yet table stakes the way the other three are.

3. Metrics: counters, gauges, histograms

There are essentially three metric types, and choosing wrongly between them produces graphs that are worse than no graph, because they look authoritative.

A counter only goes up. Requests served, bytes written, errors returned. You never read a counter's absolute value — it's meaningless, an artefact of how long the process has been running. You read its rate: the derivative. This is why counters survive restarts gracefully; the collector sees the value reset to zero and treats it as a new series rather than as a catastrophic negative spike.

A gauge goes up and down. Queue depth, connections in use, memory resident, temperature. Gauges are instantaneous samples, which means they miss everything between samples. A gauge scraped every 15 seconds cannot see a queue that spikes to 50,000 and drains in 3 seconds. This is a real and common blind spot: your queue depth graph is flat and your users are timing out.

A histogram counts observations into buckets. This is how you get latency distributions, and it is the type most people use least well.

Histogram buckets are chosen in advance, forever A histogram with buckets at 5 ms, 10 ms, 25 ms, 50 ms, 100 ms, 250 ms, 500 ms, 1 s tells you how many requests fell in each range — nothing more. If your real p99 is 1.4 seconds, every request above 1 s lands in the overflow bucket, and any p99 your query engine reports is an interpolation across a bucket with no upper bound. It will be confidently wrong. Pick buckets that bracket the latencies you actually care about, and revisit them when the service changes.

The compensation for that awkwardness is enormous: histograms are aggregatable. You can add the bucket counts from 400 instances together and compute a fleet-wide p99 that is genuinely correct, because you're summing counts, not averaging summaries. That property is worth a great deal, and it's exactly the property that pre-computed percentiles lack — which brings us to the most common statistical error in this entire field.

4. Why the average lies

Take a service handling 100 requests. Ninety-nine of them complete in 20 ms. One takes 2 seconds.

The arithmetic Mean = (99 × 20 ms + 1 × 2,000 ms) ÷ 100 = 39.8 ms.
Your dashboard shows a healthy 40 ms average. One user in a hundred waited two seconds. At 10,000 requests per second, that "one in a hundred" is 100 people every second — 360,000 people per hour — having a visibly broken experience that your primary latency graph cannot represent.

This is why latency is measured in percentiles. p50 (the median) is the typical experience. p99 is the experience of your unluckiest 1%. p99.9 is where the pathological cases live — the cold caches, the garbage collection pauses, the retries, the one shard that's rebuilding an index.

Two things about percentiles trip up almost everyone:

You cannot average percentiles. If instance A reports a p99 of 100 ms and instance B reports a p99 of 300 ms, the fleet p99 is not 200 ms. It is not any function of those two numbers. The p99 of a combined population depends on the full shape of both distributions and how many requests each served. The only correct approach is to aggregate the underlying histogram buckets and compute the percentile once, on the combined data. Every dashboard that shows "average p99 across hosts" is showing a number with no meaning.

The user's experience is worse than your p99. A single page load might make 20 backend calls. If each has an independent 1% chance of being slow, the chance that at least one is slow is 1 − 0.99²⁰ ≈ 18%. Your service's p99 is one in a hundred; your page's p99 is nearly one in five. This is why per-request percentiles flatter you and why measuring at the edge — where the user actually is — tells a different and more honest story than measuring inside a service.

Coordinated omission Gil Tene's name for the most insidious measurement bug there is. A load generator that sends a request, waits for the response, then sends the next one stops sending requests while the system is stalled. The stall produces one slow sample instead of the thousand slow samples a real user population would have experienced, because real users don't politely pause their traffic while you recover. The result is latency numbers that can be off by orders of magnitude at the tail — reported p99s of 50 ms where the true figure is seconds. The fix is to measure against the schedule on which requests should have been sent, not the schedule on which they happened to go out. HdrHistogram exists largely to make this correction possible.

The practical consequence: if your load test says p99 is 40 ms and production says p99 is 900 ms, do not assume production is misconfigured. Check whether your load generator was coordinating with the thing it was supposed to be stressing.

5. RED and USE

Two mnemonics, each for a different kind of thing, and between them they cover most of what a new service needs on day one.

RED is for services — anything that handles requests. Rate (requests per second), Errors (how many failed), Duration (distribution of how long they took). Three metrics per endpoint. If you instrument nothing else, instrument these, because almost every user-visible symptom shows up in one of them.

USE is for resources — CPUs, disks, network links, connection pools, thread pools. Utilization (percentage of time busy), Saturation (how much work is queued and waiting), Errors. Brendan Gregg's insight was that saturation, not utilization, is the signal that matters: a disk at 100% utilization with an empty queue is fine — it's working. A disk at 70% utilization with a queue 200 deep is in trouble.

Saturation is the one people forget Utilization saturates at 100% and then stops telling you anything. Every level of overload beyond that point looks identical on a utilization graph. Saturation keeps counting: queue depth, pool waiters, run-queue length, load average. When utilization pins at 100%, saturation is the only signal still carrying information about how much trouble you're in — and the only one that shows the problem growing before the utilization graph flattens.

Google's four golden signals — latency, traffic, errors, saturation — are RED plus saturation, and are as good a starting list as any. The important thing is not which acronym you adopt but that you cover both shapes: the request-handling view and the resource view. A service can be failing because it's broken (RED shows it) or because something underneath it has run out (USE shows it), and the two demand completely different responses.

One refinement worth making early Split latency by outcome. A fast failure and a fast success are both fast, and mixing them means an outage that turns 30% of your traffic into instant 500s will make your latency graph improve. More than one team has watched p99 drop during an incident and briefly felt good about it.

6. Logs: structured, sampled, expensive

Logs are the signal everybody starts with and almost nobody plans for. The failure mode is not that they don't work — it's that they work fine at 100 requests per second and become a budget line item at 10,000.

Work the numbers. Ten thousand requests per second, one log line per request, 1.2 KB per line: that is 12 MB/s, or roughly 1 TB per day, from one service. Ingest-and-index pricing for hosted log platforms is commonly quoted in the region of a few tenths of a dollar per GB, so a terabyte a day lands somewhere in the mid five figures per month — for one service, before anyone adds debug logging to chase a bug and forgets to take it out.

The debug-logging ratchet Every incident ends with someone adding more logging so that next time they'll have the data. Nobody's incident ever ends with someone removing logging. Log volume only goes one direction unless you actively push back, and the push-back has to be someone's explicit job.

Structured logging is the single highest-leverage change available. A log line like Failed to process order 88213 for user 4471 after 3 retries is readable by a human and opaque to a machine — you cannot filter it, group it, or count it without writing a regex that will break the next time someone rewords the message. The same event as JSON, with event, order_id, user_id, retry_count and duration_ms as fields, is queryable. You can ask "show me the p95 retry count grouped by shard for the last hour" and get an answer in seconds.

Once logs are structured, the line between "logs" and "metrics" blurs usefully. A sufficiently rich structured event, with high-cardinality fields attached, lets you compute the metric you wish you'd defined — after the fact, during the incident. That is the core argument for event-based observability: rather than deciding in advance which aggregations matter, keep the events and aggregate on demand.

Sampling is how you make that affordable, and the naive version is a trap. Sampling 1% of all logs uniformly means you keep 1% of your errors too — and errors are rare and precious. The workable pattern is dynamic sampling: keep 100% of errors, 100% of slow requests, 100% of anything touching a customer you care about, and 0.1% of the boring successful ones. Record the sampling rate on every retained event so you can multiply back up and still get correct totals.

Three fields worth putting on every line A trace ID (so a log can be joined to the trace it belongs to), a request ID (so a customer's support ticket can be tied to actual events), and the deploy or build version (so "did this start with the release?" takes ten seconds to answer rather than forty minutes).

7. Traces: spans and propagation

Metrics told you the checkout endpoint is slow. Logs told you what one request did inside one service. Neither tells you that the slowness lives three hops down, inside a call your service makes to a service that calls a service. That's what tracing is for.

A trace is one request's complete journey. A span is one unit of work within it — a service handling the request, a database query, an outbound HTTP call. Spans nest: the parent span's duration contains its children's. What makes it work is context propagation: the trace ID is passed along with every outbound call, so that work happening in a different process on a different continent still knows which request it belongs to. The W3C traceparent header is the standard way to carry it, and OpenTelemetry is the standard way to produce it.

◈ In the wild · Google Dapper Tracing a request across a fleet of services this way is not a new idea — it is essentially Google's Dapper, described in a 2010 paper. Dapper stamps every incoming request with a trace ID and carries it along each service call and RPC the request touches, exactly the propagation described above, and it samples aggressively so the overhead stays negligible. Zipkin, Jaeger, and the OpenTelemetry tooling most teams reach for today are all descendants of that design.
trace 4f9a2c… POST /checkout 2,410 ms ├─ gateway 2,410 ms │ ├─ auth.verify 12 ms ok │ ├─ cart.get 31 ms ok │ ├─ pricing.quote 2,301 ms ← the whole problem │ │ ├─ tax.lookup 18 ms ok │ │ └─ fx.rates 2,270 ms 3 attempts, 2 timed out at 750 ms │ └─ order.create 58 ms ok

Read that trace and the diagnosis writes itself: the currency service is timing out, the pricing service is retrying it three times, and the retries are serial. Nobody's error rate went up — every one of those spans eventually succeeded — which is precisely why the metrics dashboard stayed green. The retry policy converted an availability problem into a latency problem, and latency problems hide better.

Traces make retry cost visible, and almost nothing else does A downstream service with a 5% timeout rate and three retries looks perfectly healthy from both ends: the caller succeeds, the callee returns 200s. The cost lands entirely in the tail of someone else's latency distribution. See The Reliability Toolkit for why the retries are configured that way and what to do instead.

Sampling is unavoidable — storing every span of every request at scale is prohibitive — and where you decide is the interesting question. Head sampling makes the keep-or-drop decision at the start of the request, before anything has happened. It's cheap and simple, and it throws away the interesting requests at exactly the same rate as the boring ones. Tail sampling buffers all the spans and decides after the request completes, so you can keep every trace that errored or exceeded a latency threshold and 0.1% of the rest. It costs memory in the collector and adds a delay before traces are queryable, and it is almost always worth it.

The other half of the value is structural rather than diagnostic: a trace is an empirical, always-current service dependency graph. The architecture diagram in the wiki is a claim about how the system works; the trace data is a measurement. They differ more often than anyone is comfortable with.

8. Cardinality: the bill that ate the budget

Time-series databases store one series per unique combination of metric name and label values. Not one per metric — one per combination. That word does all the damage.

Start with something reasonable. http_requests_total labelled by endpoint, status, region and instance:

The multiplication 20 endpoints × 5 status classes × 3 regions × 100 instances = 30,000 series. Entirely fine.
Now someone adds user_id, because per-user latency would be useful. With 2 million users:
30,000 × 2,000,000 = 60 billion series. Your metrics backend either falls over or bills you as though you asked for that, because you did.

The insidious part is that the explosive labels rarely look explosive. user_id is obvious in hindsight. Less obvious: a raw URL path, where /orders/88213 and /orders/88214 are different label values and every order in your database becomes a permanent time series. Or an error message string, where a message containing a timestamp or an ID generates a new series per occurrence. Or a pod_name label in a Kubernetes cluster that redeploys twenty times a day, quietly adding a fresh set of series each time and never retiring the old ones — churn, the cardinality problem's slower cousin, where nothing looks alarming at any instant but the retained series count climbs all month.

The rule A metric label must be bounded and small — ideally under a hundred possible values, and never a value that comes from user input or a database identifier. If you want to slice by something unbounded, that is a job for logs, traces, or exemplars, not for a label. Anything you'd need a WHERE id = for belongs in an event, not a metric.

The right answer to "I want per-customer latency" is usually not a metric at all. It's structured events with the customer ID as a field, queried on demand — the cost is storage that scales with traffic rather than a cross-product that scales with your customer table. Exemplars are the useful bridge: a histogram bucket carries a handful of sample trace IDs, so you can click from "the p99 bucket got fatter" straight to an actual slow trace, without ever putting a high-cardinality value in a label.

9. SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

Everything so far has been about seeing. This section is about deciding what "working" means, in a number, in advance — which is the part that turns telemetry into a management tool rather than a hobby.

An SLI (service level indicator) is a measurement: the proportion of requests that succeeded in under 300 ms. An SLO (objective) is a target for it: 99.9% of requests, measured over 30 days. An SLA is an SLO with money attached and lawyers involved, and should always be looser than your internal SLO — you want to be paging yourself well before you're paying customers.

What 99.9% over 30 days actually buys you 30 days = 43,200 minutes. 0.1% of that is 43.2 minutes of budget.
At 10,000 requests/second, 30 days is 25.92 billion requests, so the budget is 25.92 million failed requests.
Add a nine and it's 4.32 minutes. Take one away and it's 7.2 hours. Each nine is roughly an order of magnitude more engineering, forever — which is why "let's just do five nines" is a sentence that should be met with a cost estimate rather than a nod.

The error budget is the inversion, and it's the genuinely clever idea: if your SLO is 99.9%, you are permitted 0.1% failure. That budget is a resource. Spend it on risky deploys, migrations, chaos experiments, an aggressive feature launch. Reliability stops being a vague virtue that competes with shipping and becomes a quantity that both sides of the argument can point at. Budget remaining? Ship. Budget exhausted? Freeze features, spend the sprint on stability. The policy has to be agreed before the budget is gone, or it is not a policy — it is a negotiation you will lose during an incident.

Two design decisions determine whether an SLO is useful or theatre. First, measure from the user's side: at the load balancer or in the client, not inside the service, or you will systematically exclude the failures where the request never arrived. Second, pick a window and stick to it. A rolling 30-day window is standard; calendar-month windows produce an unhelpful psychological reset on the first of the month, where the team that burned everything on the 29th is suddenly flush again on the 1st.

10. Alert on symptoms, not causes

An alert should mean: a human needs to do something, now. Everything else is a dashboard, a ticket, or a weekly report. Most alerting systems fail because they were built on the opposite principle — page on every cause anyone could think of — and the result is not vigilance but noise.

The arithmetic of noise is brutal. Sixty alert rules, each with one false positive per month, is two pages a day, forever. Nobody stays sharp under that. The team learns, correctly and rationally, that pages are usually nothing, and the response time on the page that is something quietly stretches from three minutes to twenty.

Symptom vs cause, concretely Cause alert: "database CPU is above 90%." Might be fine. Might be a batch job. Wakes someone at 3 a.m. to look at a graph and go back to bed.
Symptom alert: "checkout success rate is below 99% and falling." Always matters. Fires for database CPU, and for the seventeen other things you didn't anticipate.

The refinement on top of symptom alerting is burn-rate alerting, which asks not "are we failing right now" but "at this rate, will we blow the budget." A burn rate of 1 means you'll consume exactly your budget over the SLO window. A burn rate of 14.4 sustained for an hour consumes 2% of a 30-day budget — because an hour is 1/720 of thirty days, or about 0.139%, and 14.4 × 0.139% ≈ 2%. That combination is the standard fast-burn page: severe enough to be real, short enough to catch quickly.

Pair it with a slow-burn rule — something like a burn rate of 3 sustained over six hours — routed to a ticket rather than a page. The fast rule catches the outage. The slow rule catches the steady 0.5% error rate that no single moment makes urgent but that will exhaust your budget by the 20th. Requiring both a short and a long window to be in violation before firing is what keeps a thirty-second blip from waking anyone.

The 3 a.m. denominator problem At peak, a 5% error rate is thousands of failures and unambiguously real. At 3 a.m. you serve two requests, one fails, and your ratio-based alert reports a 50% error rate and pages someone. Every rate-based alert needs a minimum-volume floor: fire only if the error ratio is breached and the absolute count of failures exceeds some threshold. Otherwise your alerting gets loudest exactly when your traffic is quietest.

11. One incident, three signals

Here is the whole toolkit used the way it's meant to be used — the cold open at the top of this guide, worked through properly.

The page. Not "CPU high." The symptom alert fires: checkout success rate has fallen below the SLO threshold and the one-hour burn rate is 20×. Something a customer would notice is happening now.

Metrics, to bound the problem. Request rate: normal. Error rate: 0.02%, unchanged — so requests are not failing. Latency: p50 flat at 20 ms, p99 up from 240 ms to 2.4 seconds. That combination is the signature of a tail problem, not a broken system. Slice the p99 by region: all regions. By instance: all instances. By endpoint: only checkout. Four queries have eliminated most of the hypothesis space — it isn't a bad host, it isn't a bad region, it's something specific to one path.

Traces, to locate it. Pull the slow traces (tail sampling kept them all, which is the entire justification for tail sampling). Every one shows the same shape as the trace in section 7: pricing.quote holding 2.3 seconds, fx.rates inside it burning 2.27 of those across three attempts, two of which timed out. You now know where. You do not yet know why.

Logs, to explain it. Filter the currency service's structured logs by the trace ID. Every timed-out call has upstream: "fx-provider-eu" and a connect duration at exactly the 750 ms timeout. Group by deploy_version: this began at 09:02, which is when the currency service picked up a config change that moved its provider endpoint from a regional address to one that now resolves to a different continent. The physics did the rest.

The shape to internalise Metrics bounded the problem in about ninety seconds. Traces located it. Logs explained it. Each signal did the job it is cheap at, and no signal was asked to do a job it is bad at. The forty minutes lost in the cold open were spent asking metrics a question only traces could answer.

12. The gotchas that actually bite

Missing data is not zero. When a service dies, it stops reporting. A graph of its error rate goes flat, not up. An alert on "errors > threshold" will never fire, because there are no errors — there is nothing at all. Alert on absence explicitly, and give your alerting pipeline a heartbeat: a rule that fires if it hasn't heard from itself recently. A monitoring system that goes down silently is worse than none, because you're still trusting it.

Your scrape interval is your resolution limit. A 15-second scrape cannot see a 3-second spike. Queue depth, connection pool exhaustion and GC pauses all operate below that resolution. If a gauge is flat while users are timing out, suspect the sampling rate before you suspect the users.

Fast failures make latency graphs improve. If a bug turns 30% of traffic into instant 500s, your p99 gets better. Always split latency by outcome, or your headline graph will actively mislead you during the exact incident it exists for.

Dashboards with auto-scaled y-axes. Every graph looks dramatic when the axis fits the data. A 0.3% wobble rendered full-height reads as a catastrophe; a real doubling on a fixed axis reads correctly. Pin the axis to something meaningful, and put the SLO threshold on the graph as a line so the eye has a reference.

Clock skew makes traces impossible. Spans carry timestamps from the hosts that produced them. A host 400 ms out of sync produces a child span that appears to start before its parent, and a waterfall that no amount of staring will make sensible. Run NTP and monitor the offset — it's a two-line metric that will one day save you a day.

Averaged percentiles, one more time. If a dashboard panel says avg(p99), it is displaying a number that corresponds to no property of your system. Aggregate the histogram buckets, then compute the percentile once.

Logs contain what you put in them, including the things you shouldn't. Email addresses, tokens, full request bodies with card details. Logs are usually the least access-controlled datastore in the company and the most widely replicated. Redact at the emitting library, not in a downstream pipeline, because the pipeline will be bypassed by someone debugging at some point.

Instrumenting only the paths you own. A request that never reached your service produces no telemetry in your service. TLS handshake failures, DNS problems, gateway 502s and client-side errors are all invisible from the inside. If your SLI is computed from application logs, it structurally cannot count the requests that never arrived — and those are exactly the ones customers complain about.

13. Recap in 60 seconds

  • Monitoring answers known questions; observability answers new ones. The failure that takes you down is the one nobody put on the dashboard.
  • Three signals, three jobs. Metrics say that something is wrong (cheap, but cardinality-bound). Traces say where (sampled). Logs say why (linear in traffic, therefore expensive).
  • Averages hide everything that matters. 99 requests at 20 ms and one at 2 s averages to a healthy-looking 40 ms. Use percentiles — and never average percentiles across hosts; aggregate buckets instead.
  • Watch saturation, not just utilization. Utilization stops carrying information at 100%; queue depth keeps counting.
  • Cardinality is a multiplication. Labels must be bounded and small. Never a user ID, a raw URL path, or anything from user input. Use events and exemplars for the unbounded stuff.
  • An SLO turns reliability into a budget. 99.9% over 30 days is 43.2 minutes. Spend the budget deliberately; agree the freeze policy before you need it.
  • Alert on symptoms and burn rate, never on causes. Add a minimum-volume floor so 3 a.m. arithmetic doesn't page anyone.
  • Missing data is not zero, and a monitoring system that fails silently is worse than none.

14. Check yourself

During an incident your p99 latency graph improved. What's the most likely explanation?
Something started failing fast. A dependency that returns a connection-refused in two milliseconds is far quicker than one that does real work, so a bug converting a third of your traffic into immediate 500s will pull the whole latency distribution down and make the graph look better than it did yesterday. The same effect shows up when a circuit breaker opens — that's the breaker working correctly, but it means latency alone can no longer tell you anything about health. The fix is to split every latency metric by outcome and to make the primary panel show successful requests only, with error rate directly beside it. A latency graph that mixes successes and failures is measuring two different populations and reporting their blend as though it were one number.
Your queue-depth gauge is flat at near zero, but consumers are visibly falling behind and users are timing out. Where's the gap?
A gauge is an instantaneous sample, so a 15-second scrape interval can only ever see 15-second-apart snapshots of a value that may be moving far faster. A queue that fills to 40,000 and drains in four seconds is completely invisible — every scrape happens to catch it empty. Anything bursty needs either a counter you can take the rate of (enqueued and dequeued totals, whose divergence reveals the backlog even between samples) or a high-water-mark gauge that the application resets each scrape rather than reporting the current value. The general lesson is that your scrape interval sets a hard floor on the duration of any phenomenon you can observe, and most of the interesting ones — GC pauses, pool exhaustion, lock contention — live below it.
Product wants per-customer latency, and you have 50,000 customers. Do you add a customer_id label?
No — that multiplies every existing series by 50,000 and will either take down your metrics backend or produce a bill that gets the whole observability programme cancelled. The correct home for an unbounded dimension is an event store: structured logs or traces with customer_id as a field, queried on demand, where cost scales with traffic rather than with the cross-product of every label. If you need alerting rather than ad-hoc querying, the usual compromise is to promote a bounded subset — a tier label with three values, or an explicit allow-list of your twenty largest accounts, which keeps the cardinality fixed and known. Exemplars close the remaining gap by attaching a few trace IDs to each histogram bucket, so you can jump from an aggregate to a specific customer's slow request without any high-cardinality label existing at all.
Two instances report p99s of 100 ms and 300 ms. What's the fleet-wide p99?
Unknowable from those two numbers. A percentile is a property of a distribution, and you cannot recover the combined distribution from two summary points — the answer depends on the full shape of both and on how many requests each instance served. If the 300 ms instance handled 1% of traffic the fleet p99 is close to 100 ms; if it handled 60% it is much nearer 300 ms; and in a skewed population the true value can sit outside the range of the two inputs entirely. The only correct method is to sum the histogram bucket counts across instances and compute the percentile once on the combined counts, which is exactly why bucketed histograms are worth their awkward fixed boundaries. Any dashboard showing avg(p99) or max(p99) across hosts is displaying a number with no defined relationship to user experience.
You sample 1% of traces at ingress to control cost. Why do rare errors stay invisible?
Head sampling decides before the request runs, so it cannot know the request will be interesting — it keeps 1% of everything, which means 1% of your errors too. An error occurring in one request per hundred thousand now appears roughly once per ten million, which in practice means never, and when you finally get a report you'll find you have no trace for it. Tail sampling fixes this by buffering all spans until the request completes and deciding afterwards: keep 100% of errors, 100% of anything over a latency threshold, and a fraction of a percent of the routine successes. The costs are real — the collector needs memory to hold in-flight traces, and traces become queryable a few seconds later than they otherwise would — but they buy you the property that the traces you kept are the ones you'd actually want, which is the entire point of keeping any.
Your metrics pipeline dies at 2 a.m. Every graph goes flat and no alert fires. Why, and what would have caught it?
Because almost every alert is written as a threshold on a value — errors above N, latency above M — and when a series stops arriving there is no value to compare, so the rule evaluates to nothing rather than to a breach. Absence looks like calm. Two mechanisms cover it: an explicit staleness rule that fires when a series you expect has reported nothing for some multiple of its scrape interval, and a dead man's switch, which is an alert deliberately wired to fire continuously into an external service that pages you if the stream stops. The second is the one that catches a total pipeline failure, because it does not depend on the pipeline being alive to work — and the general principle it embodies is that anything monitoring itself has a blind spot exactly the size of itself.
Your load test reports a p99 of 40 ms; production is at 900 ms with the same traffic shape. Name the most likely measurement bug.
Coordinated omission in the load generator. A tool that sends a request, waits for the response, then sends the next one stops issuing load exactly when the system stalls — so a two-second freeze produces one slow sample instead of the thousands of slow samples that real users, who do not pause politely, would have generated. The stall is recorded as a single unlucky request rather than as a period during which everyone was queued, and the tail of the reported distribution is understated by orders of magnitude. The correction is to measure each request's latency against the time it should have been sent under the intended arrival rate, not the time it was actually dispatched; HdrHistogram's correcting recorders exist to do precisely this. Before rebuilding a service to chase a discrepancy like this, check whether the two numbers are even measuring the same thing.
